# DigitalCo: The 14-Week CIO Arc
## Story, Decisions, Scoring, and the Traps That Spread the Field

DigitalCo is a regional industrial-equipment manufacturer in the Caterpillar mold, sized at the Timken or Lincoln Electric scale (a few billion in revenue, focused product lines, a dealer and distributor channel, and an installed base of increasingly connected machines in the field). The new CIO inherits a stalled connected-products initiative, a dual-ERP mess, a data swamp, and a factory floor that IT can't see into. The organizing question runs end to end: DigitalCo makes excellent machines, but its competitors are turning the same machines into data and services businesses, and the board is split between the heritage camp that wants to run the business and the transformation camp that wants to bet on data and AI. Across fourteen weeks the team decides whether and how to make data and AI the thing rivals can't replicate.

This document lays out the arc. Each week has a focus, what it teaches, the decision the team faces, how it's scored, and the twist or trap waiting for groups who take the easy path. The competition runs underneath all of it.

---

# The competition and scoring architecture

Every team is the CIO of its own DigitalCo, playing the identical arc in parallel. They're scored continuously and ranked against each other, and the rivalry is real in two ways at once. The meta-game is the leaderboard, revealed at four phase boundaries so teams always know where they stand. The in-world game is a set of named rival manufacturers whose publicized moves create herd pressure and tempt teams into traps, so the competition lives inside the story and not just on a scoreboard.

Four dimensions accumulate across the sim. Strategic Judgment asks whether the decision was sound given what was knowable that week. Execution and Consequence Management asks whether the team handled the fallout, the stakeholders, and the second-order effects. Coherence asks whether each decision aligns with the team's prior choices and its stated strategy, and it's where zigzagging gets punished. Deliverable Quality covers the memo, analysis, or deck, and it's the dimension that carries most of the MIS versus MISX difference in weight and depth.

On top of the running score sit gates. Certain catastrophic omissions cap a team's endgame ceiling no matter how well they score elsewhere, because that's how real IT leadership works. The clearest one is the security and OT through-line: a team that treats security as a Week 10 problem instead of a fourteen-week discipline can't reach the top tier once the breach hits, because the neglect is already baked in. Gates give the traps teeth and stop a team from buying its way out of a structural mistake with strong deliverables.

The endgame resolves to one of four tiers, following the Cybersecurity Executive model. Triumph, Win with scars, Squeak through, and Disaster are a function of total accumulated score and gate status. The leaderboard then ranks teams within and across tiers, so two teams can both land at Win with scars and still finish meaningfully apart. The four benchmark reveals fall at the end of Weeks 4, 8, 11, and 14, and the traps are calibrated so that each reveal spreads the field a little wider.

The trap philosophy is consistent enough that students eventually learn to feel it coming, which is the point. The seductive move, the one that pleases the board or matches what a rival just did or makes the team look decisive, is usually the trap. The harder, less flashy, more coherent move is usually right. A team that internalizes this by Week 14 has learned the actual lesson of the sim, which is that coherent strategy beats topic-by-topic optimization.

---

# The four phases

Phase 1, Weeks 1 through 4, is Inheritance and Foundations. The CIO assesses the mess, survives the alignment fight, triages a failing project, and makes the core architecture call. The first benchmark lands after Week 4.

Phase 2, Weeks 5 through 8, is Strategy and Data. The CIO reads the disruptive landscape, decides whether a platform is real or a fantasy, weathers a vendor crisis built on an earlier choice, and turns the data swamp into the asset the whole strategy depends on. The second benchmark lands after Week 8.

Phase 3, Weeks 9 through 11, is The Bets Come Due. The AI bet, the security breach, and the data-rights crisis arrive in sequence, and the earlier through-lines either pay off or detonate. The third benchmark lands after Week 11.

Phase 4, Weeks 12 through 14, is Reckoning and Synthesis. The cloud bill comes home, the CIO pitches the accumulated strategy to a skeptical board, and the arc resolves into an integrated position and a tier outcome. The final benchmark and leaderboard land after Week 14.

# Week 1 — Onboarding and inherited assessment

The focus is the new CIO's first thirty days. The team reads the inherited environment and the politics, finds the stalled connected-products initiative, the legacy IBM i running order management underneath a half-finished S/4HANA migration, the data swamp, and the OT black box on the factory floor. This week teaches current-state assessment, IT capability maturity, stakeholder mapping, and the discipline of telling symptoms apart from root causes. The decision is what to prioritize and, more pointedly, whether to make early visible moves to establish credibility or to hold and diagnose at the risk of looking passive.

Scoring rewards thorough diagnosis and accurate root-cause identification, and it penalizes premature commitment and being captured by the loudest stakeholder. The strategy statement a team writes here becomes its public commitment, the yardstick that every later coherence score measures against, so a vague or sloppy Week 1 quietly handicaps a team for the whole sim. The trap is the urge to do something to seem like a leader, killing the connected-products initiative on day ten because it's bleeding money or committing to finish S/4 to please the CFO, before anyone understands the dependencies. There's a planted misdirection too: the most articulate and confident executive gives a compelling but self-serving read, and teams who trust charisma over evidence start the arc pointed slightly wrong.

# Week 2 — IT and business alignment confrontation

The heritage-versus-transformation split erupts, and the CIO has to align IT strategy with a business that doesn't agree with itself. The week teaches alignment models, IT governance, the CIO as translator, and the danger of an IT strategy disconnected from business strategy. The decision is whose vision to back and how, siding with the heritage CFO on cost and execution, the transformation CEO on the data and services bet, or sequencing both into a defensible third path, and then building a governance structure to make the choice stick.

Scoring rewards a real position paired with a governance mechanism, and it penalizes pure people-pleasing, because trying to satisfy everyone produces a strategy with no edge that reads as incoherence rather than wisdom. The trap is exactly that seductive third way that turns out to be mush. The genuine skill is making a choice and governing around it, not splitting the difference. The planted twist is political subtext: the CEO privately wants the bold bet but won't say so publicly, and the test is whether the team can read that and give the CEO the cover to commit. Coherence to Week 1 starts mattering here, and the first benchmark is now visible on the horizon.

# Week 3 — Failing project crisis

The S/4HANA migration hits the wall. A major milestone is blown, the overrun is exposed, the business is in revolt, and the board wants answers. The week teaches troubled-project recovery, sunk-cost reasoning, the difference between rescuing, restructuring, descoping, and killing, and the management of vendors and systems integrators under pressure. The decision is which of those paths to take and how to communicate it, owning it transparently or spinning it, and whether to fire the integrator.

Scoring rewards sound sunk-cost reasoning, a credible recovery or kill plan, and transparent communication, and it penalizes throwing good money after bad and blame-shifting the board can see through. The trap is sunk cost itself: a team that has already spent forty million rationalizes finishing because killing feels like admitting failure, when the correct move is often to descope and stabilize, or to kill. There's a planted lifeline, a quick fix the vendor offers that looks like salvation but deepens lock-in and pushes the real problem to Week 12, so teams who grab it pay later. A gate opens here, because teams who pour money into the failing project without a recovery plan damage their budget and credibility and start capping their own ceiling.

# Week 4 — Make versus buy versus rent

The core architecture decision arrives: the data and IoT platform that underpins the whole data-and-services bet gets built in-house, bought as a vendor product, or rented from a hyperscaler. The week teaches make-buy-rent economics, total cost of ownership, the core-versus-context distinction (build what differentiates, rent what doesn't), and the opening moves of cloud and lock-in economics. The decision is the platform sourcing call plus the first explicit cloud commitment, one hyperscaler, multi-cloud, or on-prem and edge.

Scoring rewards a TCO-grounded decision that builds the differentiating layer and rents the commodity layer, and it penalizes both build-everything, the trap for ambitious teams who fall for not-invented-here and run out of resources, and rent-everything-blindly, the trap for teams who ignore lock-in. Coherence with the Week 2 alignment is checked, because a team that backed the bold data bet and then under-invests here is contradicting itself. The deeper trap is set for later: the hyperscaler that offers the most generous upfront terms, the credits and the free migration, is the one that sets the deepest lock-in, and it detonates in Week 7 and again in Week 12. This is the end of Phase 1, and the first benchmark, an industry analyst ranking the field on IT credibility and strategic positioning, reveals how far the early traps have already spread the standings.

# Week 5 — Disruptive technology read

The CIO has to assess emerging technology, autonomy, digital twins, additive manufacturing, and edge AI, and separate what's real from what's hype, what threatens the core from what's noise. The week teaches disruptive innovation theory, the incumbent's dilemma applied to a manufacturer, technology scouting, and real-options thinking. The decision is which technologies to bet on, pilot, watch, or ignore, how much to commit, and whether to stand up a separate innovation capability or embed it in the line.

Scoring rewards a disciplined split between genuine disruption and vendor hype and a portfolio of bets rather than an all-in or all-out posture, and it penalizes both hype-chasing and the incumbent complacency of dismissing everything. The trap is the demo dazzle: the most impressive pitch, the slick digital-twin or autonomous-everything show, is usually the least ready and the least relevant to DigitalCo's actual position, while the genuinely disruptive technology arrives looking boring. The planted twist is competitive: a named rival very publicly bets on the dazzling technology, and the test is conviction against herd behavior. Teams who follow the rival into the hype trap misallocate; teams who hold to their own analysis pull ahead.

# Week 6 — Platform and network effects

The question is whether DigitalCo can turn its connected-equipment ecosystem into a platform with real network effects, a marketplace or data exchange or ecosystem play, or whether that's a fantasy that has killed better companies. The week teaches platform strategy, network effects, two-sided markets, and the specific failure mode of industrial platform envy, with the GE Predix story as the cautionary case. The decision is whether to pursue a platform and what kind, how much to invest, and whether to open the ecosystem to dealers and third parties or keep it closed.

Scoring rewards a sober read of whether network effects actually exist for DigitalCo, which is usually weaker than it looks, and a right-sized play, and it penalizes both platform envy, declaring a grand ecosystem with no network-effect engine, and the timidity that cedes a genuine opportunity. The trap is the platform the board wants: directors have read about platforms and crave one, and the seductive move is to announce a grand DigitalCo ecosystem that burns money like Predix did. The harder, more correct read is usually a focused connected-features-and-services play rather than a platform. The planted twist loads a future crisis: opening the platform to dealers could create modest real network effects but ignites the data-ownership fight that explodes in Week 11, so this choice pre-positions that crisis.

# Week 7 — Vendor crisis

A key vendor detonates. The hyperscaler from Week 4 raises prices or gets acquired, or a critical supplier fails, and the lock-in from earlier choices comes due. The week teaches vendor risk management, switching costs, multi-sourcing, contract and SLA management, and concentration risk, with the running theme that yesterday's convenient choice is today's exposure. The decision is how to respond, renegotiate, switch at great expense, absorb the hit, or accelerate a hedge, and how to communicate the crisis to the board.

Scoring rewards teams who built optionality earlier and respond with a measured hedge, and it punishes teams trapped by the Week 4 sweet deal who now have no options and must eat a brutal squeeze. This is the first detonation of the lock-in trap, and the obvious response, switching vendors immediately, is often wrong because the switching cost is exactly the trap. There's a second, quieter twist: a faint signal that the connected fleet has a security exposure surfaces the same week, and teams fixated on the vendor fire miss the security smoke. The security and OT gate tightens here. Teams who have ignored OT through seven weeks and miss this signal have now effectively locked in a ceiling below Triumph for the Week 10 breach, and the field begins to split between the teams quietly building security posture and the ones who keep deferring it.

# Week 8 — Data and analytics strategy

The data swamp has to become an asset, which means governance, ownership, architecture, and the central question of turning installed-base telemetry into the competitive advantage the whole strategy rests on. The week teaches data strategy, data governance, data as an asset, analytics maturity, and the genuinely contested question of who owns the machine data, the company, the dealer, or the customer who bought the equipment. The decision is the governance model, the data-rights posture, the analytics architecture, and how aggressively to assert ownership against dealers and customers. This is the keystone of the data-and-services spine.

Scoring rewards a governance model that makes the data usable while navigating the ownership politics without starting a channel war, and it penalizes both governance neglect, which leaves the swamp in place, and the over-aggressive land grab that detonates the dealer relationship early. Coherence with the whole spine is weighted heavily here, because this is the week the strategy either becomes real or stays a slogan. The trap is the data-rights land grab: asserting ownership of all machine and customer data looks like building advantage, but it pre-detonates the Week 11 crisis and risks a customer revolt of the right-to-repair kind, while teams who duck the ownership question entirely arrive at the benchmark with no advantage to show. This is the end of Phase 2, and the second benchmark, a competitive-position readout on who's actually building data advantage, moves the leaderboard sharply as the spine-coherent teams pull clear.

# Week 9 — AI adoption decision

The AI bet arrives, predictive maintenance on the connected fleet, computer-vision quality on the line, generative design, AI agents in dealer and parts support, alongside the governance of shadow AI and the GenAI vendor-concentration question. The week teaches AI strategy, make-buy-rent for AI specifically, GenAI vendor concentration and hyperscaler entanglement, shadow-AI governance, and the difference between AI theater and AI value. The decision is where to deploy AI for genuine advantage, whether to build models or rent foundation models, how to govern the shadow AI already running everywhere, and how much to concentrate on a single GenAI vendor.

Scoring rewards AI deployed where it compounds the data advantage, which is predictive maintenance on proprietary telemetry, and a sober governance regime, and it penalizes AI theater, ungoverned shadow AI, and blind single-vendor concentration. Coherence with Week 8 is structural rather than optional, because AI advantage requires the data foundation, so teams who neglected the data strategy literally can't extract full value here. The trap is AI theater: the board wants an AI strategy, and the seductive move is to sprinkle generative AI across everything for visible activity, when the real value is narrow and deep and far less flashy. The planted twist is a shadow-AI incident mid-week, an engineer who leaked design IP into a public model or a dealer chatbot that gave legally problematic advice, testing whether the team has governance or gets caught flat.

# Week 10 — Security breach

The breach hits, and given the spine, the sharpest version is an OT and connected-fleet attack: ransomware that stops a plant, or a compromise of fielded machines that can be bricked or hijacked from afar, or both at once. The week teaches incident response, OT and IT security convergence, crisis management under uncertainty, breach communication and disclosure, and the board and regulatory dimensions. The decision is the incident response under pressure, contain, communicate, disclose, decide on the ransom, and manage the board, customers, dealers, and regulators simultaneously, all of it a compound crisis rather than a single clean problem.

Scoring rewards teams whose earlier OT investment limited the blast radius and who run a disciplined, transparent response, and it heavily penalizes teams who hit the OT gate and now face a fleet-wide catastrophe with no visibility and no plan. This is where the gate set back in Week 7 is enforced, and the trap was set across the entire sim: a team that treated security as a Week 10 topic instead of a through-line is now defenseless. Inside the week the crisis compounds, the breach coinciding with a board member leaking to the press, a key security person quitting, and a ransom deadline, so teams who handle it linearly get overwhelmed. The ransom decision is its own trap with no clean answer, scored on reasoning rather than the binary. The field splits dramatically here, and this is the single biggest leaderboard mover, the clearest proof that a coherent through-line beats box-checking.

# Week 11 — Customer data and privacy strategy

The data-rights fight explodes into the open. Dealers and customers contest who owns the machine data, a right-to-repair pressure campaign builds, a regulatory or legal angle appears, and the channel relationship strains under the weight of earlier choices. The week teaches data privacy and rights in a B2B context, the right-to-repair movement, contracts and data terms, and the tension between data advantage and customer and channel trust. The decision is how to resolve the ownership conflict, concede rights to preserve the channel, hold firm and risk a revolt, or craft a shared-value model where data sharing benefits dealers and customers too, and how to handle the public pressure and any regulatory threat.

Scoring rewards teams who navigate to a shared-value resolution that preserves both the data advantage and the channel trust, and it penalizes both the teams now reaping the Week 8 land grab, who face a full revolt, and the teams who concede everything and lose the advantage the whole strategy was built on. The Week 6 platform-openness choice and the Week 8 data-rights choice both come due here in a designed convergence. The trap is the symmetry trap: both the aggressive pole and the timid pole lose, and teams gravitate naturally to one or the other, when the skill is the harder middle. The planted twist is a customer or dealer going public or legal mid-week, a lawsuit or a viral campaign, forcing a response under pressure that earlier choices have left either defensible or exposed. This is the end of Phase 3, and the third benchmark reads stakeholder trust and competitive position, separating the teams who built advantage without burning trust from the teams who overreached or caved.

# Week 12 — Cloud and infrastructure decision

The cloud bill comes home. The telemetry from the connected fleet generates enormous cost, the Week 4 lock-in constrains the options, and the edge-versus-cloud economics for the fleet have to be resolved. The week teaches cloud economics at scale, data gravity, edge computing, repatriation, FinOps, and the long-run consequences of the original sourcing decision. The decision is the infrastructure architecture for the fleet at scale, how much to push to the edge, whether to repatriate any workloads, how to control the bill, and whether to restructure the hyperscaler relationship.

Scoring rewards teams whose lock-in-aware Week 4 choice and Week 7 hedge now give them cost control and architectural options and who right-size the edge-versus-cloud split, and it punishes teams trapped by lock-in facing an unmanageable bill with no way out. This is the third detonation of the Week 4 trap. The deeper trap is data gravity: teams who centralized everything in one cloud for convenience now face a bill that grows with the fleet and data that can't easily move, which locks them in further. The planted twist is a tempting committed-spend discount that lowers the bill now while deepening lock-in for the future, the same seduction as Week 4, testing whether the team learned from the earlier trap or falls for it twice. Teams who learned pull ahead on efficiency and optionality; teams who keep taking the convenient deal compound their disadvantage.

# Week 13 — Board communication on a major bet

The CIO presents the accumulated strategy, the data-and-services bet, the AI investment, the platform decision, to a skeptical and divided board, and asks for a major commitment, the funding and mandate and go-or-no-go on the equipment-as-a-service transformation. The week teaches executive and board communication, business-case construction, the language of risk and return and strategic narrative, and the management of a hostile audience. The decision is what to ask for and how to frame it, the scope of the bet, the narrative, the risk framing, and the size of the ask, while handling the heritage camp's skepticism and the CFO's cost concern. This is largely a deliverable week, the board deck, and it's where MIS and MISX assessment diverge most.

Scoring rewards a coherent strategic narrative grounded in the accumulated decisions, a credible business case, and skilled handling of skepticism, and it penalizes incoherence above all, because a pitch that contradicts earlier weeks now has nowhere to hide, and the whole sim's coherence is audited here. The trap is the incoherence reckoning: teams who optimized each week locally without a through-line have no coherent story to tell, and it shows under questioning. The injected twist is a live hostile question, a heritage director attacking the premise or the CFO challenging the return, testing whether the team defends its strategy or folds. The board's mood is itself a function of accumulated state, so a team that mishandled Week 10 faces a board that has lost confidence while a team that built trust faces a receptive room, which makes earlier performance shape the difficulty of the ask. If run as live presentations to a faculty or peer panel, this is the marquee competitive moment.

# Week 14 — Integrative strategic positioning

The endgame asks where DigitalCo ends up, a machine company that bolted on some software or a genuinely data-and-AI-advantaged industrial company, and the CIO articulates the integrated position and the path forward. The week teaches strategic synthesis, the integration of every thread (governance, architecture, data, AI, security, platform, cloud, alignment), and the meta-lesson that coherent strategy beats topic-by-topic optimization. The decision is less a single choice than a synthesis, the final positioning statement and forward strategy, set against a reckoning with everything the team has accumulated.

Scoring is the endgame computation: total accumulated score across all four dimensions, gate status, and coherence, resolving into Triumph, Win with scars, Squeak through, or Disaster. The final integrative synthesis is weighted heavily and is where MIS analytical depth most diverges from MISX. The final benchmark and leaderboard land here, and teams receive both their tier and their competitive rank alongside a debrief that traces how the through-line, or its absence, produced the result. The final twist is the debrief reveal: teams see how their small early choices, the Week 1 strategy statement, the Week 4 lock-in deal, the Week 8 data-rights stance, the OT neglect, propagated into the endgame in ways they didn't track in real time. The trap across the whole sim was always the same one, treating each week as an isolated decision instead of one continuous strategy, and the teams who felt that coming finish at the top.

# MIS versus MISX in the scoring

The engine, the scenarios, the advisors, and the four-tier outcome are identical across both courses, and the simulation never forks. What forks is the wrapper. Graduate MIS teams play with less advisor scaffolding, more ambiguous tradeoffs, and heavier analytical deliverables each week, an executive memo, a strategic synthesis, and the occasional board deck, and their Deliverable Quality dimension carries more weight and is graded for analytical depth and defensibility. Undergraduate MISX teams play with more advisor guidance, clearer decision sets, and explicit framework labeling tied to the classic syllabus, and their deliverables are lighter, with the framework application graded as much as the conclusion. The traps and through-lines are the same for both, but MISX gets more signposting toward them while MIS is left to feel them unaided.

# What to build next

The arc, the scoring, and the competition model are here. The natural next pieces are the six advisor agent character briefs, the voices and lanes and signature concerns and the specific thing each one notices that the others miss, since the advisors are how students actually experience each week's decision. After that, the named board and exec roster as characters, then a deeper specification of the MIS and MISX deliverable rubrics week by week, and then the detailed scenario scripting for Week 1 as the first buildable module for the fall pilot.
