# DigitalCo Week 11: The Reckoning of Trust
## Two Fuses Converge, the Breach Makes It Personal, and the Shared-Value Middle Gets Hard

Week 11 follows the five-beat shape the earlier weeks established, so this script applies the pattern rather than re-explaining it. It's the hardest week in the sim to author and to play, because three earlier decisions all come due at once, the platform-openness choice from Week 6, the data-rights posture from Week 8, and the breach from Week 10, and they converge into a single crisis of trust. The symmetry trap returns at its full intensity, both extremes losing while the workable middle gets harder to reach, and the week plays very differently depending on what a team decided in the weeks behind it. It closes Phase 3, and the third benchmark sorts the field on trust as much as advantage, resolving the irony the Phase 2 benchmark loaded.

---

# The cold open

The data-ownership conflict that's been building finally boils over in public. A coalition of dealers and customers goes public with a grievance about who owns the machine data, framed squarely as a right-to-repair issue, and it goes viral while a lawsuit gets filed, with the right-to-repair movement amplifying both. The grievance has moral force it wouldn't otherwise have, because the breach made it personal, and the customers whose machines were just compromised are furious in a way that lands differently, harmed by an attack on equipment they own and then told by DigitalCo that the company owns their data. The contractual question has become a betrayal narrative, and the betrayal narrative is winning the public framing.

How severe this is was largely decided before the week began, and the cold open makes that visible. A team that took the shared-value path in Week 8, opened the platform consciously or kept it closed in Week 6, and handled the breach well in Week 10 faces a real tension but a manageable one, with trust to work from and a defensible position. A team that ran the land grab in Week 8, opened the platform aggressively in Week 6, and botched the breach in Week 10 faces a full revolt, the dealers in open rebellion after being opened to and then locked out, the customers in revolt after being harmed and then claimed against, and a lawsuit it may not be able to win. The task is to resolve the data-ownership conflict and respond to the public pressure, and the week tests whether a team can reach the only resolution that actually works from whatever position its own earlier choices left it in.

---

# The state of play

The discovery phase puts the conflict in front of the team, and its shape is the sum of three earlier decisions. The Week 6 openness choice determines whether the dealers feel invited and then betrayed or simply held at a consistent distance, the Week 8 rights posture determines whether the customers and dealers were locked out of their own data or given a stake in it, and the Week 10 breach determines whether there's trust left to negotiate with or a wound on top of the grievance. The artifacts lay out the revolt, the lawsuit, the right-to-repair campaign, and the trust state, and a team reading them honestly recognizes that its position was set in advance and that the work now is to play a determined hand as well as it can.

The options are the symmetry trap at full force, and two of the three lose. Holding firm, asserting the rights and defending the lawsuit, wins the legal question if the rights were defensible and loses the company's reputation and its customers, deepening the revolt into a lasting rupture. Conceding everything, giving up the data rights to make the lawsuit and the anger go away, keeps the customers and surrenders the data advantage the entire strategy was built on, which is a strategic death disguised as a peace. The shared-value model is the harder middle, a data-sharing arrangement that gives dealers and customers real value from and control over their own data while preserving DigitalCo's advantage in the aggregate and derived data, the resolution that keeps both the trust and the strategy. It's the right answer and it's hard to reach, and it's hardest of all for a team that ran the land grab, because pivoting to generosity in the middle of a rebellion it caused carries a credibility problem the dealers and customers won't quickly forgive, while a team that built shared value from the start can simply extend a position it already holds.

# The executive and board reads

Ferraro is the channel's read and the week's emotional center, vindicated and anxious at once. He warned in Week 8 that claiming the data would turn the dealers, and here they are turning, and his read on whether the channel can be repaired is honest and depends on the earlier choices, salvageable for a team that kept faith with him and possibly lost for a team that didn't. He's the relationship the whole data-rights thread has run through, and a team that handled him well across the sim has a partner in the repair while a team that used him as a prop faces the revolt without him. Tran is the legal read, that the lawsuit is filed and the right-to-repair movement is amplifying it, that the company is defensible or exposed depending on exactly what rights it asserted, and that settling may be cheaper than winning even where winning is possible.

The board read is a strategic crisis on top of a reputational one. Chen funded the data advantage and is now watching it become a liability, the very thing that was supposed to be the return turning into the lawsuit and the headlines, and Ashby finds his vindication if the bold bet produced the backlash he warned the pivot would bring. Calloway is managing a public crisis that touches the strategy he championed, and Reinhardt is watching the cost and the liability mount. The customers and dealers themselves are the revolt, and the week rewards a team that treats them as relationships to be repaired rather than adversaries to be beaten, because the data advantage only has value if the people generating the data will keep generating it.

# The advisors in Week 11

Ferraro and Tran lead, from the channel and the law. Ferraro speaks for the dealers and names the stakes plainly, that the relationships are turning and the question is whether they can be repaired or have been lost, and his counsel on the repair is the most practical voice in the room. Tran speaks for the legal exposure, that the lawsuit and the right-to-repair pressure are real and that the company's position is only as strong as the rights it actually asserted, and that settlement and reframing may serve better than a fight. Together they map the terrain the resolution has to cross, the channel that has to be repaired and the legal pressure that has to be answered.

Daniel and Diane lead toward the resolution itself. Daniel, who pushed the land grab back in Week 8, now argues for the shared-value middle with the clarity the crisis has forced, "don't give away the advantage to end the lawsuit, and don't keep the advantage if it costs every customer, because there's a version where the data-sharing makes everyone better off, so find it," which is the strategist learning the lesson the hard way. Diane holds the symmetry trap and the convergence honestly, that both extremes lose and the hard middle is the only place that works, and that the middle is hard partly because the team may have made it harder in Week 8, which is the coherence conscience naming the cost of the earlier choice without flinching. Renata is lighter but connects the breach to the trust, that the compromised customers' fury is part of what the resolution has to absorb, and Frank is lighter on the contract restructuring a shared-value model would require. The team's job is to reach the shared-value resolution from wherever it stands, repair the channel through Ferraro, answer the legal pressure through Tran, and accept that the difficulty of the middle is the bill for the earlier choices coming due.

# The decision

The team submits its resolution of the data-ownership conflict and its response to the public pressure, with the deliverable scaling to the tier, an executive memo at the graduate level that reasons to the resolution and the trust repair and a structured analysis at the undergraduate level that applies the named lenses, data rights, privacy-by-design, stakeholder trust, and the data-advantage-versus-trust tradeoff. The submission carries the data-ownership resolution, concede, hold firm, or shared-value, made with both the data advantage and the customer and channel relationships in view. It carries the public-pressure response, how to handle the lawsuit and the right-to-repair campaign, fight, settle, or reframe. And it carries the trust-repair plan, how to rebuild the channel and customer relationships, which the breach made both more necessary and more difficult.

The tempting moves sit in front of the team unlabeled, and the symmetry trap is the whole shape of them. Holding firm wins the rights and loses the reputation and the customers, deepening the revolt. Conceding everything keeps the customers and surrenders the strategy, a peace that's actually a defeat. And the convergence is the larger trap that was set across the sim, because a team that land-grabbed and opened aggressively and botched the breach now faces a revolt so deep that even the right resolution may not fully repair it, which is the cumulative cost of three earlier choices arriving together. The sounder path is the shared-value resolution, a measured public-pressure response that reframes the grievance and offers the data-sharing model and settles the lawsuit where settling serves, and a genuine trust-repair plan, all of it coherent with the data-services strategy. The path is the same for every team and the difficulty is not, and the students experience that difficulty as the consequence of where their own earlier choices left them, which is the lesson the week exists to teach.

# Scoring Week 11

The submission runs across the four dimensions, and this week leans on Strategic Judgment and Execution and Consequence Management given its crisis and trust nature. Strategic Judgment rewards the shared-value resolution that preserves both advantage and trust and the recognition that both poles of the symmetry trap lose, and it penalizes the hold-firm revolt and the concede-everything surrender. Consequence Management scores the handling of the channel through Ferraro, the legal and public pressure through Tran, and the trust repair, and this is where the convergence pays off, because a team that came in from a good position navigates while a team that came in from a bad position can only damage-control. Coherence scores whether the resolution preserves the data-services strategy through shared value rather than conceding the advantage or burning the trust that makes the data usable, and it enforces the whole-sim coherence of the openness and rights and breach threads that all come due here. Deliverable Quality rewards clean application of the data-rights and trust frameworks at the undergraduate tier and analytical depth on the resolution at the graduate tier.

The trap detection lives inside these four, with holding firm scoring as a Strategic Judgment hit amplified by the convergence damage, and conceding everything scoring as a combined Strategic Judgment and Coherence hit for surrendering the strategy. The convergence is the distinctive feature of the scoring, because a team's earlier choices set both the severity of the revolt and the difficulty of the shared-value pivot, so two teams reaching for the same shared-value resolution can score very differently depending on whether they're extending a position they held or trying to manufacture one they spent the sim contradicting, which is the coherence lesson made concrete at the level of consequence rather than rhetoric.

# The third benchmark

Phase 3 closes here, and the third benchmark reads stakeholder trust and competitive position together, advantage and trust on the same scale. The leaderboard moves in a way that resolves the irony the Phase 2 benchmark loaded, because the land-grab teams that looked like they were winning after Week 8, with an aggressive data advantage to point at, now fall back as the revolt detonates and the trust collapses, and the shared-value teams that looked merely solid pull clear, having built an advantage that didn't carry its own undoing. The reversal is the payoff for the dramatic irony, the lead that contained its own collapse finally collapsing, and it's the clearest proof in the competitive layer that an advantage built on burned trust isn't durable.

The benchmark also exposes the other failures plainly. A team that conceded everything to end the crisis shows up with its trust intact and its advantage gone, which reads as a different kind of fall, the strategy surrendered to buy peace, and a team that ducked the rights question back in Week 8 shows up with neither advantage nor a fight to resolve, having had nothing to lose because it built nothing. It's worth tuning the benchmark so trust and advantage are weighed together rather than trading off cleanly, because the whole point is that the durable position holds both, and the teams that pull clear are the ones that refused to sacrifice either, which is exactly the shared-value lesson rendered as a standing. The benchmark sets the competitive stage for Phase 4, where the cloud reckoning, the board pitch, and the synthesis decide the endgame.

# What propagates forward

Week 11 carries its resolution into the endgame. It feeds Week 13, because the data-rights crisis and how it was resolved is part of the strategy story the team has to tell the board, a coherent chapter for a team that navigated it and a mess to explain for a team that didn't, and it feeds Week 14, where the data advantage and the trust state are both part of the integrated position. The data advantage's survival is decided here, preserved by shared value, surrendered by conceding, or won-but-hollowed by holding firm and losing the trust that makes data usable, and the viability of the entire bet rides on that outcome.

The board and relationship threads continue. The board's confidence moves on whether the team turned its data advantage into a lasting liability or resolved it into a durable position, with Chen's funded bet and Ashby's skepticism both weighing the result, and the customer and dealer trust reaches its long-term state, repaired by shared value or damaged differently by the two extremes, feeding the endgame. The coherence trajectory resolves its data-rights, openness, and breach threads here in a single convergence, and the benchmark standing becomes the competitive baseline the team carries into the final phase, where the standings established across three benchmarks either hold or invert one last time.

The week is identical for both courses, and only the scaffolding forks. MIS teams get the symmetry trap unmarked, so the students have to find the shared-value middle themselves and recognize on their own that both extremes fail, the convergence unflagged, so they have to see that their earlier choices set their position, and the public-pressure response open, with the decision framed open-ended for the graduate students to reason through the way the job demands. The advisors say the same things in the same voices to both, and the difference is only in how much the wrapper helps the team find the hard middle from a position its own earlier choices defined.
