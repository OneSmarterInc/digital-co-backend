# DigitalCo Week 2: The Alignment Confrontation
## The First Coherence Test, and the Week Calloway's Subtext Comes Due

Week 2 follows the five-beat shape Week 1 established, the cold open, the discovery phase, the decision, the scoring, and the consequences, so this script applies that pattern rather than re-explaining it. What makes Week 2 distinct is that it carries two jobs nothing before it could. It's the first week the coherence dimension has a prior week to measure against, since the Week 1 strategy statement is now the yardstick, and it's the week Calloway's private wish for the bold bet comes due, the subtext a sharp team caught in Week 1 and now has to act on. The week is built around a single hard truth, that a CIO can't align a business with itself by making everyone happy.

---

# The cold open

The heritage-and-transformation tension that simmered under Week 1 breaks into the open, and it breaks in a specific room. Calloway has to take a strategic direction to the upcoming board meeting, and he's told the CIO plainly that he needs the executive team aligned behind one IT strategy he can present, because walking into that board with a divided team in front of Chen and Ashby is a position he won't take. So he convenes a strategy session to produce that aligned direction, and the session fractures almost immediately. Reinhardt pushes to stabilize the core, finish or cleanly kill S/4, and prove a return before another dollar goes into the digital bet, while the pull toward transformation, carried by Chen's pressure and the logic of the installed-base opportunity, pushes to commit to the data-and-services direction now. The room splits, and the most important thing in it is what Calloway does, which is nothing. He sits back and watches the CIO, and he doesn't referee.

That non-intervention is the cold open's real signal. Calloway is testing whether the CIO can read what he actually wants and produce a direction he can get behind, rather than waiting for him to declare himself, which he won't do in front of a divided room. The stakes are concrete, the board meeting is coming, Chen expects to see a direction, and Calloway needs an aligned strategy he can champion rather than a personal gamble he has to defend alone. The task lands on the CIO to produce an IT strategy that aligns with the business, which forces the three questions the week is about, which business vision the strategy backs, how to build governance that makes the choice hold instead of collapsing back into faction-fighting, and how to position the whole thing so Calloway can carry it to the board.

---

# The state of play

The discovery phase in Week 2 is about reading the political landscape rather than the technical one, and the artifacts are the competing positions and the machinery, or absence of machinery, for resolving them. Two rival strategy plans are on the table for the team to examine, a stabilize-and-optimize plan that Reinhardt's thinking shapes, all cost discipline and core stabilization and deferred ambition, and a transform plan that commits to the data-and-services direction with the spend and the risk that implies. The plans are drawn as opposites on purpose, because the trap is to split the difference between them, and a team that reads them as a menu to average from is already heading wrong.

The artifact that matters most is the one that's missing, which is real IT governance. Bryce ran IT by personality and ad hoc deal, which is part of why the digital bet stalled with no one accountable, so the company has no standing mechanism for making IT strategy decisions, no steering structure, no stage-gates, no legitimate way for the factions to engage a decision rather than fight it in the hallway. A team that notices the governance vacuum understands the week faster than a team that treats this as purely a which-side-do-I-pick question, because the durable answer isn't only a direction, it's a direction plus the structure that makes it stick and gives Reinhardt his discipline and the business units their voice without surrendering the strategic choice.

---

# The executive and board reads

Calloway is the read the entire week turns on. In conversation he stays publicly careful, talking about balance and prudence and the board's expectations, but the team that listens past the words catches that he wants the bold bet and is waiting for someone to hand him a version of it he can survive championing. He won't say this, and he'll let the CIO take the risk of proposing it, and the tell is consistent with Week 1, that his caution is a position he's holding rather than a conviction he holds. A team that takes his public prudence at face value misreads him and plays safe, and a team that hears the subtext gives him something to step toward.

The rest of the cast holds the positions the roster set. Reinhardt is the brake, pushing stabilization and proof, not from hostility but from being burned, and he's persuadable by a strategy that gives him financial discipline through stage-gates rather than asking him to trust another vision. Chen, pressing from the board side, wants the digital bet to move and is running out of patience, which is the transformation pull and the clock at once. Ashby anchors the heritage worry, wary of what the pivot does to the company's identity, and he's the reason the bold bet has to be made politically survivable rather than declared. Petrillo wants to know what any of this means for her floor and stays skeptical of grand strategy that lands as disruption, Ferraro is quietly alert to whether the direction threatens his channel, and Lena Fischer is watching whether the alignment treats the connected-products work as hers or as something IT is annexing. None of them is neutral, and a team that reads the alignment question as a clean strategic choice rather than a negotiation among interests misses what's actually in the room.

# The advisors in Week 2

This is Diane Brandt's week as much as Week 1 was, because the work is political before it's strategic. She presses the team to separate what Calloway said from what he wants, asking whether they've read the man or only his words, and she's blunt about the mush, "if you try to make all of them happy, you'll build a strategy with no spine, and they'll all be unhappy by spring." She's the coherence conscience too, and she'll put the question directly, whether the alignment they're converging on still matches the thesis they committed to in their first week, because she remembers it even if the team has started to drift. Her counsel is the antidote to both the indecision trap and the misread of Calloway, and a team that leans on her without also hearing the others can tip into reading the politics so carefully that it never commits.

The others work their lanes around her. Daniel Stern makes the strategic case for committing to the data-and-services direction and aligning the company behind it, which is the right destination, but his bias pulls toward declaring the bet boldly and fast without enough weight on the political feasibility, the cover Calloway needs and the discipline Reinhardt demands, so a team captured by Daniel gets the direction right and the survivability wrong. Marcus Webb pushes on what governance would actually function and warns against aligning around a strategy the architecture can't deliver, which keeps the choice honest. Renata Voss is quieter but holds the through-line, noting that whatever direction wins the alignment fight, the factory-floor exposure doesn't get resolved by a governance decision and is still sitting there unfunded, which is the early warning that survives every week until Week 10. Frank Delgado and Zoe Park are lighter here, Frank noting that a coherent direction strengthens the company's hand in the vendor decisions ahead and Zoe offering that a vivid future-state vision can help sell the transformation to the board, with the usual discount on the hype.

# The decision

The team submits an aligned IT strategy with a governance mechanism, and the deliverable scales to the tier, an executive memo or strategy brief at the graduate level that states the position and the governance and reads the politics, and a structured alignment-and-governance analysis at the undergraduate level that applies the named lenses, the strategic grid, the alignment models, and the governance structures, to reach and defend the same choices. Three things have to be in it. The first is an alignment position, a genuine choice of which business vision the IT strategy backs and why, and the bar is that it's a real choice rather than a balanced non-answer. The second is a governance mechanism, the standing structure for how IT strategy decisions get made going forward, the thing that gives Reinhardt his stage-gated discipline and the business units a legitimate voice while keeping the strategic direction coherent, and the thing that makes the alignment durable instead of something the company re-litigates every week. The third is the political positioning, how the strategy is framed so Calloway can champion it to the board, which is where reading his subtext turns into action.

The tempting moves sit in front of the team unlabeled, and the central one is a trap. The balanced strategy that gives Reinhardt his cost discipline and Chen her transformation and Ashby his heritage and Ferraro his channel, all at once, looks like wisdom and statesmanship and lands as mush, a direction with no edge that satisfies no one for long and reads as indecision. The two ways of mishandling Calloway are traps of their own. Taking his public caution at face value and proposing the safe stabilization play misses what he actually wants and leaves him with nothing to step toward, forfeiting the chance to convert a powerful watcher into a patron, while pushing the bold bet raw, without the governance and staging that make it survivable against the heritage board, hands Calloway a gamble he can't sell to Ashby and Sokolski and exposes him rather than covering him. The sounder path is to make the genuine choice, almost certainly the data-and-services direction if the team committed to it in Week 1, and to wrap it in a governance structure that stages and disciplines it so the heritage camp gets the core protected and Reinhardt gets his gates, and to package it as the executive team's aligned recommendation that Calloway can own publicly rather than as his personal bet. The students experience these as open options, and the scoring is what teaches the line between a chosen sequenced path and a mushy split.

# Scoring Week 2

The submission runs across the four dimensions, and this is the week coherence stops being a baseline and starts being a measurement. Strategic Judgment rewards a genuine, defensible alignment choice paired with a governance mechanism that would actually hold, and a correct read of what Calloway wants, and it penalizes the balanced non-answer, the direction proposed with no structure to make it stick, and the misread that takes Calloway's caution literally. Execution and Consequence Management scores the handling of the factions, whether the team gave Reinhardt discipline and the heritage camp reassurance and the business a voice, and above all whether it gave Calloway cover he can carry to the board, and it penalizes a team that wins the strategy argument on paper while leaving the key people unmanaged.

Coherence is the dimension that newly bites. The team's Week 2 alignment is measured against the Week 1 strategy statement, so a team that declared a data-and-services thesis and now backs stabilization, or mushes the choice, takes a real coherence hit, while a team that visibly extends its Week 1 commitment scores well. There's a compounding effect worth naming, that a team whose Week 1 anchor was itself vague now has little to be coherent to, so the earlier handicap surfaces here as a strategy with nothing firm to align against, which is exactly how incoherence propagates forward. Deliverable Quality rewards clean application of the alignment and governance frameworks at the undergraduate tier and analytical depth on the alignment choice and the governance design at the graduate tier, and the trap detection lives inside these four rather than beside them, so the mushy third way shows up as a combined Strategic Judgment and Coherence hit, the clearest signal the week produces.

---

# What propagates forward

Week 2 installs the machinery the rest of the sim runs decisions through. A team that built real governance now has a structure for making later choices stick and a legitimate channel for the factions to engage, while a team that chose a direction without governance, or chose nothing, will re-fight alignment in some form almost every week, which is a quiet tax on everything ahead. The Calloway relationship is the other large consequence, because a team that read his subtext and gave him cover converts him from a careful watcher into a patron who carries weight when it counts in Week 13, and a team that misread him leaves him uncommitted or exposed and forfeits that ally.

The smaller balances carry forward too. Reinhardt moves further toward the CIO if the governance gave him genuine financial discipline and stays a brake if his cost concern was waved past, and the postures of Ferraro, Petrillo, and Fischer shift by whether the alignment respected or threatened their interests, seeding the channel fight in Weeks 8 and 11, the OT relationship that decides Week 10, and the connected-products cooperation in Weeks 5 and 6. Most consequential of all is the coherence trajectory, because Week 2 is where a team either confirms it's building one continuous strategy or starts to zigzag, and that trajectory compounds straight through to the coherence audit in Week 13 and the synthesis in Week 14.

# MIS versus MISX in Week 2

The week is identical for both courses, and only the scaffolding forks. MISX teams get the frameworks named, the strategic grid and the alignment and governance models offered as the lenses for the work, the factions' positions drawn more clearly, and a real hint that Calloway's public position may not be his private one, so the undergraduates learn to read political subtext with the possibility of it flagged for them. MIS teams get none of that, with Calloway's subtext unmarked so the students have to catch that his caution is a mask on their own, the factions' self-interest unflagged, and the decision framed open-ended without the option set drawn, so the graduate students do the political read and the governance design the way the actual job demands. The advisors say the same things in the same voices to both, and the difference is only in how much the wrapper helps the team hear what Calloway isn't saying.

Week 2 puts coherence to work and resolves the Calloway thread, and it hands the team a governance structure, or the lack of one, that shapes every decision after it.
