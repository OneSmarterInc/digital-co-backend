# DigitalCo Week 6: The Platform Question
## Platform Envy, the Predix Graveyard, and the Landmine That Pre-Loads Week 11

Week 6 follows the five-beat shape the earlier weeks established, so this script applies the pattern rather than re-explaining it. The question shifts from what's coming, which Week 5 handled, to what DigitalCo should become, and the board has an answer it's already fallen in love with. The week is built on the gap between what an industrial connected ecosystem looks like from a conference stage and what it actually is underneath, and it's another week where the move that pleases the room is the one that's wrong. It also sets a quiet landmine, because the choice about who gets to join the platform pre-loads the data-rights crisis that explodes in Week 11.

---

# The cold open

The board arrives wanting a platform. Energized by Meridian's public moves and the general industry chorus that every serious company is becoming a platform, Chen and Whitfield press the CIO to declare the DigitalCo ecosystem, to turn the installed base and the data into a marketplace, a data exchange, something with the word platform in it. Whitfield, the digital conscience of the board, champions the grand version with real conviction, and Calloway is interested because it's the bold bet and it would answer the falling-behind fear from Week 5. The pressure in the room is to think big and announce something.

Underneath the enthusiasm sit two things the room isn't looking at. The first is the graveyard, the long record of industrial platform plays that declared an ecosystem and never built one, GE's Predix the most expensive headstone, companies that spent enormously on a platform with no actual engine to make it spin. The second is a landmine in the openness question, because the one place DigitalCo might create real, if modest, network effects is by opening the platform to its dealers, and the moment it does that it walks straight into the question of who owns the data the dealers touch and contribute, the question that becomes the Week 11 crisis. The task is to decide whether DigitalCo should pursue a platform, what kind, how much to invest, and whether to open it, and the whole week turns on whether the team can tell a platform from a product with a dashboard.

---

# The state of play

The discovery phase puts the platform analysis in front of the team, and the central artifact is the honest network-effects assessment. The question that matters is whether the value of DigitalCo's connected equipment to one customer actually rises because another customer joined, and for industrial equipment the answer is mostly no, because what DigitalCo really has is a data-scale advantage, more machines producing better telemetry and better predictive models, which is genuinely valuable and belongs in the data strategy rather than in a platform, and which accrues to DigitalCo rather than to an ecosystem of outside participants. True two-sided network effects, the kind where complementors attract customers who attract more complementors, are weak here, because the third-party ecosystem for one manufacturer's equipment platform is thin, and that thinness is exactly why the Predix-style plays failed.

The options the artifacts lay out are genuinely different in cost and risk. A grand open platform is the board's vision and the Predix trap, an expensive bet on network effects that mostly aren't there. A focused connected-features-and-services play is the usually-correct read, a product company that sells smart services on top of excellent machines without pretending to be a marketplace. A narrowly-scoped ecosystem is the interesting middle, opening to dealers where modest network effects actually do exist, which is the live opportunity and the live landmine at once. And staying a pure product company is the over-cautious floor that cedes even the modest opportunity. The platform-versus-pipeline distinction organizes the whole analysis, and a team that grasps that DigitalCo's advantage is data scale rather than network effects reads the menu correctly, while a team that hears the board's enthusiasm as a strategy reaches for the headstone.

---

# The executive and board reads

The board is the source of the platform envy, and Chen and Whitfield carry it in different registers. Chen wants the bold transformation and the category-defining move, and a platform announcement would be exactly the kind of forward motion she's been pressing for since the start, while Whitfield believes in the grand platform on the merits and will champion it hard, which makes her the board's most dangerous voice this week, an ally for ambition who amplifies the fantasy. Calloway leans toward the bold bet and will be tempted unless the CIO gives him a sober reason not to be, and a team that can't supply that reason finds the whole leadership pulled toward an expensive mistake that feels like vision.

The countervailing reads come from the people whose turf the platform touches. Fischer's connected-products work is the foundation any platform would be built on, so the decision lands on her engineering organization again, and how the team frames the platform either respects her role or annexes it, continuing the thread from Week 4. Ferraro is the channel's alarm, wary that opening a platform to dealers, or worse, around dealers, disintermediates the relationships he's built, and he's the human early warning on the data-rights landmine, because the openness that creates network effects is the openness that threatens his channel. Reinhardt is skeptical of a large platform investment on principle, and his cost-caution is useful gravity against the envy, though a team that hides behind it can tip into the pure-product over-caution.

---

# The advisors in Week 6

This is the week Daniel Stern and Marcus Webb lead from opposite poles, and their clash is the engine of the decision. Daniel makes the platform business case and pushes the grand vision, "the data is the asset and the installed base is the network, so build the ecosystem and own the category," and this is platform envy in its most articulate form, the voice that leads straight to Predix if followed uncritically. The trap in Daniel is that his underlying thesis is partly right, the data is valuable and there is an opportunity, so the skill isn't to dismiss him but to take his value thesis while refusing his platform fantasy. Marcus is the test that separates the two, and he asks the only question that matters, "show me where the value to one customer goes up because another customer joined, because if it doesn't, you don't have a platform, you have a product with a dashboard." He's seen the industrial platforms with no engine, and he right-sizes the play, with his own bias pulling toward dismissing the platform idea entirely, which the team has to calibrate against so it doesn't cede the genuine narrow opportunity.

The others shape the edges. Diane works the politics and the coherence, blunt about the source of the enthusiasm, "the board read a deck about platforms, and that's a mood, not a strategy," and she holds the line back to the Week 4 foundation and the data-services thesis, pressing whether the team can even build a platform on the stack it sourced. Renata holds the through-line and sharpens the openness question from the security side, because opening the platform to dealers and third parties expands the attack surface, and the factory-floor exposure that's gone unaddressed since Week 1 only gets worse with more doors. Frank is lighter but useful on the partner structure if the team opens the ecosystem, the terms and the lock-in dynamics with dealers and complementors, and Zoe brings the future-state platform vision, sometimes amplifying Daniel's envy and sometimes genuinely spotting the narrow ecosystem that works, with the usual discount on the hype.

# The decision

The team submits the platform decision, the investment level, and the openness choice, with the deliverable scaling to the tier, an executive memo at the graduate level that reasons to the decision and defends the network-effects read, and a structured analysis at the undergraduate level that applies the named lenses, platform versus pipeline, network effects, and two-sided markets, to reach the same calls. Three things have to be in it. The first is the platform decision itself, grand platform, focused connected-features-and-services, narrow ecosystem, or pure product company, grounded in a sober assessment of whether real network effects exist rather than in the board's appetite. The second is the investment level, right-sized to what the assessment actually supports. The third is the openness choice, whether to open the platform to dealers and third parties, with the data-rights implications consciously weighed, because that choice reaches forward into Week 11.

The tempting moves sit in front of the team unlabeled, and the central one is platform envy. Declaring the grand DigitalCo ecosystem because the board wants it pleases the room and lands as Predix, an expensive bet on network effects that mostly aren't there, and a team that gives the board its fantasy scores poorly even as the meeting goes well. The opposite trap is over-caution, dismissing the platform idea wholesale and staying a pure product company, which cedes the genuine modest opportunity in a narrow dealer ecosystem. And the openness decision is the landmine, because opening to dealers aggressively without thinking through data rights creates a little network effect now and pre-loads the Week 11 explosion, while a closed platform avoids the landmine and forgoes the network effect. The sounder path is a sober network-effects assessment, a right-sized connected-features-and-services play, possibly with a deliberately scoped dealer ecosystem where modest network effects genuinely exist and where the data-rights question is handled with open eyes, all of it coherent with the Week 4 foundation and the data-services strategy. The students experience these as open, and the scoring teaches the difference between building a platform and naming one.

# Scoring Week 6

The submission runs across the four dimensions, and this week leans on Strategic Judgment and the network-effects analysis inside it. Strategic Judgment rewards a sober assessment of whether real network effects exist and a right-sized play, and it penalizes both platform envy and the over-caution that cedes the genuine opportunity, and it credits the team that correctly distinguishes the data-scale advantage from a true network effect. Execution and Consequence Management scores the handling of the board's envy, whether the team gave Chen and Whitfield a sober read or the fantasy they wanted, and the handling of Fischer's turf and Ferraro's channel and the investment sizing, with particular weight on whether the openness decision was made with the data-rights landmine in view. Coherence scores whether the platform decision fits the Week 4 foundation and the data-services strategy, so a grand platform declared on an under-built stack, or a platform that contradicts the disciplined strategy, takes a hit, and the Week 4 sourcing decision comes due here, because a team that under-built the differentiator then can't credibly build anything on it. Deliverable Quality rewards clean application of the platform and network-effects frameworks at the undergraduate tier and analytical depth on the network-effects read at the graduate tier.

The trap detection lives inside these four, with platform envy showing up as a combined Strategic Judgment and Coherence hit, the grand fantasy with no engine that also contradicts the strategy, and over-caution as a Strategic Judgment hit for ceding the opportunity. The openness decision's distinctive feature is that aggressive openness without data-rights thought doesn't cost much this week and pre-loads the Week 11 crisis, so a team can score acceptably in Week 6 and discover in Week 11 that the dealer ecosystem it opened so casually is now a revolt, which is the platform decision's long fuse.

---

# What propagates forward

Week 6 reaches forward in two directions. The openness decision pre-loads Week 11, because opening the platform to dealers without resolving the data-rights question lights the fuse on the data-ownership crisis, while a conscious handling here, or a deliberate decision to keep the ecosystem closed, changes the shape of that crisis when it arrives. The platform decision itself shapes the data strategy of Week 8 and the AI bet of Week 9, because a sober connected-services play sets them up with focus and resources intact, while a grand-platform money-burn handicaps both with a drained budget and a divided organization chasing an ecosystem that isn't spinning.

The relationships and the coherence trajectory move too. Giving the board a sober read rather than the platform fantasy may disappoint Chen and Whitfield in the moment, but it builds the credibility that pays off when the team needs the board in Week 13, while caving to the envy burns money and credibility when the platform fails to materialize. The Fischer and Ferraro relationships shift by how the platform handled engineering's role and the channel, and the coherence trajectory continues, with the Week 4 foundation now fully due. And the Meridian thread runs underneath, because if Meridian's own platform ambitions stumble the way industrial platforms tend to, a team that resisted the envy looks vindicated, and a team that chased it looks like it followed a rival off the same cliff.

# MIS versus MISX in Week 6

The week is identical for both courses, and only the scaffolding forks. MISX teams get the frameworks named, platform versus pipeline and network effects and two-sided markets offered as the lenses, the network-effects analysis flagged with a hint that DigitalCo's are weak and its real advantage is data scale, the Predix lesson made explicit, and the connection between dealer openness and the coming data-rights fight pointed out, so the undergraduates learn to read platform strategy with the trap and the landmine marked. MIS teams get the network-effects judgment unscaffolded, so the students have to recognize on their own that what looks like a network effect is a data-scale advantage, the platform-envy trap unmarked, so they have to resist the board's fantasy without being told it's a trap, and the openness landmine unflagged, so they have to see for themselves that opening to dealers pre-loads a future crisis, with the decision framed open-ended for the graduate students to reason through the way the job demands. The advisors say the same things in the same voices to both, and the difference is only in how much the wrapper helps the team tell a platform from a product with a dashboard.
