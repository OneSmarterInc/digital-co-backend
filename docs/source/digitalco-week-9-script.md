# DigitalCo Week 9: The Bet
## AI Theater Versus AI Value, the Shadow-AI Incident, and the Week the Board Is Right

Week 9 follows the five-beat shape the earlier weeks established, so this script applies the pattern rather than re-explaining it. It opens Phase 3, where the bets come due, and it's the AI bet, the modern-transformation heart of the sim alongside Week 8. The week carries a deliberate break in the sim's pattern, because this is the week the board is right, and the skill is executing its sound instinct rather than resisting it. The trap isn't AI, it's the theater version of AI, and the real value sits exactly where the team's earlier data work either built a foundation or didn't.

---

# The cold open

The board wants an AI strategy, and for once the board is correct. AI is everywhere in the industry, Chen and Whitfield want DigitalCo to have a real answer, and the strategic instinct behind the demand is genuinely sound, because an industrial company that becomes data-and-AI-advantaged is exactly what the whole arc has been building toward. The pressure, then, isn't to resist the board, it's to give the board something real, and the temptation underneath that pressure is to give it something that only looks real.

Two things shape the week from the start. Shadow AI is already running everywhere and ungoverned, the engineers pasting design work into public models, the dealers spinning up chatbots, marketing generating content, exactly as it was in Week 1 and never addressed, and it's a live exposure waiting for an incident. And the genuine opportunity sits quietly in the data, because predictive maintenance on the connected fleet's proprietary telemetry is the high-value play, the AI use case that compounds the data advantage from Week 8 and that no competitor can easily copy, and it's far less flashy than a website full of copilots. The task is to decide where to deploy AI for real value, whether to build models or rent them, how to govern the shadow AI, and how much to concentrate on a single AI vendor, and the whole week turns on whether the team can tell a business from a demo.

---

# The state of play

The discovery phase puts the AI opportunity landscape in front of the team, and the items differ enormously in value even where they look similar in a demo. Predictive maintenance on the fleet telemetry sits at the top because it draws directly on the proprietary data and compounds the Week 8 advantage, the defensible, deep play. Computer-vision quality on the line and selective AI agents in dealer and parts support are real if narrower. And a wide scatter of GenAI copilots and chatbots, deployable everywhere and impressive in a demo, sit at the bottom because they compound nothing and produce visible activity rather than advantage. The AI value chain organizes the menu, and a team that reads it for where value actually accrues reaches for predictive maintenance, while a team that reads it for what looks like an AI strategy reaches for the copilots.

The other artifacts sharpen the decisions around the deployment. The shadow-AI reality is laid out plainly, the ungoverned use and the IP-leakage and liability risk it carries, and it's primed to produce an incident this week. The build-versus-rent question frames whether to build models on the proprietary data, the differentiating capability, or rent foundation models, the commodity layer, the same core-versus-context logic from Week 4 applied to AI. And the vendor-concentration question is the lock-in lesson returning, because renting AI from the same hyperscaler that just doubled the cloud bill in Week 7 deepens exactly the dependence that crisis exposed, so the team's answer here reveals whether it learned anything. The Week 8 dependency runs underneath all of it, because a team that built the data advantage can make predictive maintenance real while a team that swamped finds the high-value play has no foundation to stand on, which is the designed link between the two weeks.

# The executive and board reads

This is the week the board's read is the right one to follow, with a translation. Chen and Whitfield want AI and they're correct to, and a team that delivers genuine AI value gives Chen the return she's been waiting for and satisfies the board on the merits, which makes this the rare week where alignment with the board is the correct move. The catch is that the board's instinct is sound and its picture of AI is fuzzy, so the team has to give the board the substance behind the demand rather than the demo the demand seems to ask for, and a team that delivers theater seems to satisfy the board in the moment while giving Chen nothing real to show, which the board eventually sees through.

The other reads sharpen the value-versus-theater line. Reinhardt wants AI to produce a return rather than become another visible program with no payoff, and his demand for real value is useful pressure toward predictive maintenance and against the copilot scatter. Fischer's engineers are among the heaviest shadow-AI users, generative design being a real temptation and a real IP-leakage risk, so engineering is both an AI opportunity and an exposure. Ferraro's dealers are running the chatbots that the week's incident may center on, which ties the shadow-AI problem to the channel. Calloway watches the bet closely, because a real AI win is exactly the kind of thing that makes the Week 13 board pitch land, and a theater bet is exactly the kind of thing that hollows it out.

# The advisors in Week 9

This is Daniel Stern and Zoe Park's week to lead, and both of their biases point at theater. Daniel makes the bold AI case and pulls toward going big and deploying everywhere, "be an AI company," and Zoe brings the frontier excitement and the scatter of use cases, the copilots and agents everywhere, novelty mistaken for value. Following either uncritically lands the team in theater, and yet both carry a real signal underneath, Daniel that AI genuinely matters and Zoe that there may be a real frontier use case worth seeing, so the skill is taking the enthusiasm and refusing the breadth-for-show. Marcus is the lens that separates value from theater, blunt that a chatbot on the website is a demo and predictive maintenance on the fleet is a business, that one compounds the data advantage and one is a press release, and he applies the build-versus-rent logic to keep the differentiating models in-house and the commodity rented.

The warnings come from Renata, Tran, and Frank. Renata names the shadow-AI exposure and predicts the incident, that the engineers are leaking IP into public models right now and the team will wish it had built governance when the incident lands this week, and a team that hears her is ready while a team that doesn't is caught flat. Tran adds the legal teeth, that a dealer chatbot giving a customer bad advice the customer relies on is DigitalCo's liability and not the dealer's, so ungoverned AI is ungoverned legal risk. Frank returns the lock-in lesson, pointed about the team renting its AI from the same hyperscaler that just doubled its bill, asking whether anything was learned in Week 7 and warning that concentrating AI on one vendor hands that vendor even more leverage. Diane holds the coherence line and frames the release valve directly, that the board is right to want AI and wrong about what AI looks like, so the team's job is to give them a real return rather than a demo, which is the week's whole lesson in a sentence.

# The decision

The team submits its AI strategy, with the deliverable scaling to the tier, an executive memo at the graduate level that reasons to the deployment, the sourcing, and the governance, and a structured strategy at the undergraduate level that applies the named lenses, the AI value chain, make-buy-rent for AI, and AI governance, to reach the same calls. The submission carries the deployment strategy, where to put AI for real value, with predictive maintenance on the proprietary fleet data as the high-value core and a deliberate refusal of the everywhere-for-show scatter. It carries the build-versus-rent decision and the vendor-concentration choice, building the differentiating models and hedging the AI vendor rather than handing a single hyperscaler more leverage. And it carries the shadow-AI governance, both the response to the incident that lands this week and the regime going forward.

The tempting moves sit in front of the team unlabeled, and the central one is AI theater. Sprinkling GenAI across everything for visible activity looks like an AI strategy and produces no compounding value, and it's the move a team reaches for when it hears the board's demand as a demand for a demo, which is exactly the misread the release valve is designed to expose, because the board wanted substance and the team gave it theater. Concentrating all the AI on the same already-squeezing hyperscaler is the lock-in lesson unlearned. Leaving the shadow AI ungoverned is the move that the week's incident punishes, an engineer's leaked IP or a dealer chatbot's liability landing on a team with no governance to absorb it. The sounder path is deep AI value in predictive maintenance that compounds the data advantage, a build-versus-rent decision that owns the differentiating models and hedges the vendor, and a shadow-AI governance regime that meets the incident with a plan, all of it coherent with the data strategy from Week 8. The students experience these as open, and the scoring teaches that the AI that looks most like a strategy is often the AI that does the least.

# Scoring Week 9

The submission runs across the four dimensions, and this week leans on Strategic Judgment and the AI-value reasoning inside it. Strategic Judgment rewards AI deployed where it compounds the data advantage and a sober governance and vendor posture, and it penalizes the theater scatter, the single-vendor concentration that repeats the lock-in, and the ungoverned shadow AI. Execution and Consequence Management scores the handling of the board's appetite, whether the team delivered real value or theater, and the response to the shadow-AI incident, which functions as a governance test, because a team that built governance meets it with a plan while a team that didn't takes a reputational and legal hit. Coherence scores whether the AI bet fits the Week 8 data strategy and the spine, so predictive maintenance on the proprietary data reads as coherent while theater reads as a contradiction, and the Week 8 dependency bites here, because a swamped team can't score well on AI value it has no foundation to produce. Deliverable Quality rewards clean application of the AI frameworks at the undergraduate tier and analytical depth on the AI strategy at the graduate tier.

The trap detection lives inside these four, with theater scoring as a combined Strategic Judgment and Coherence hit, single-vendor concentration as a Strategic Judgment hit for the repeated lock-in, and ungoverned shadow AI as a Consequence Management hit when the incident lands, with a possible legal or reputational consequence that follows into Weeks 10 and 11. The release valve shapes the scoring in a way worth naming, because this is the week where the board-aligned move scores well rather than poorly, so a team that delivers the substance the board actually needs is rewarded for alignment, which is the deliberate counterexample to the weeks where the board-pleasing move was the trap.

# What propagates forward

Week 9 sets up the back third in several directions. A deep AI value play strengthens the data-services bet by compounding the advantage, while a theater bet wastes resources and leaves the strategy no stronger, and the difference shows at the Week 13 board pitch, where a real AI return is a centerpiece and a demo is a liability. The shadow-AI governance, or its absence, carries straight into Weeks 10 and 11, because a team caught flat by this week's incident has a reputational and legal mess that compounds with the security breach and the data-rights crisis, while a team that governed it is steadier going in.

The vendor and budget threads continue too. The vendor-concentration decision feeds Week 12, because AI compute concentrated on the squeezing hyperscaler deepens the lock-in the cloud reckoning will expose, and the budget and OT gate from earlier weeks shape what the team can do, with a squeezed team short on AI investment and a gated team missing the OT visibility that fleet predictive maintenance actually needs. The coherence trajectory continues, with deep AI value reinforcing the spine and theater contradicting it, and the board relationship moves on the release valve, with real value earning genuine board confidence and theater spending it.

# MIS versus MISX in Week 9

The week is identical for both courses, and only the scaffolding forks. MISX teams get the frameworks named, the AI value chain and make-buy-rent for AI and AI governance offered as the lenses, the deep-value-versus-theater distinction flagged with a hint that predictive maintenance is the real play, the shadow-AI incident surfaced more clearly as a warning to build governance first, and the vendor-concentration lock-in echo pointed back to Week 7, so the undergraduates learn to build an AI strategy with the theater trap and the governance test marked. MIS teams get the deep-value-versus-theater judgment unscaffolded, so the students have to find the real value themselves rather than being told predictive maintenance is the play, the shadow-AI incident landing more as a surprise that tests whether they governed it, and the vendor-concentration lesson unmarked so they have to remember Week 7 on their own, with the decision framed open-ended for the graduate students. The advisors say the same things in the same voices to both, and the difference is only in how much the wrapper helps the team tell a business from a demo.
