# DigitalCo Week 13: The Audit
## The Coherence Reckoning, the Marquee Deck, and the Board That Remembers Everything

Week 13 follows the five-beat shape the earlier weeks established, so this script applies the pattern rather than re-explaining it. It's the coherence audit, the climax of the through-line the sim has tracked since Week 1, and one of the four heavy-deliverable weeks, the marquee board deck where the two tiers diverge most sharply. The week turns on a single brutal fact, that a strategy either adds up or it doesn't, and the board is the place where that gets settled in public. The board's mood is not something the team manages this week, it's the accumulated weight of every relationship and every crisis across the whole arc, and the team walks into a room that remembers all of it.

---

# The cold open

The board convenes, and the CIO has to present the strategy the entire sim has been building, the data-and-services transformation that turns DigitalCo from a machine company into a data-and-AI-advantaged one, and ask for a major commitment, the funding to scale it, the mandate to drive it, and the go decision on the full equipment-as-a-service transformation that is the boldest version of the bet. The room is the board roster at full strength and full skepticism, Ashby holding the heritage line, Sokolski ready to attack the premise, Chen wanting the bet to pay off and demanding the numbers, Whitfield championing the digital case, and Hargrove sitting on the swing vote that decides the room.

What the team walks into was decided before the meeting. The board's mood is the sum of the accumulated state, so a team that handled the breach well, navigated the data-rights crisis, gave Calloway cover back in Week 2 and delivered since, and converted Reinhardt across the arc faces a board willing to listen, while a team that botched the crises, burned the relationships, and never earned its patrons faces a board that has already lost confidence and is looking for a reason to say no. Calloway is in the room, and whether he carries the pitch or sits on his hands depends on the cover the team did or didn't give him, and his own standing rides on how the presentation lands. The task is to present the accumulated strategy, frame the ask, and defend it under fire, and the week tests whether the arc the team actually played can be told as a story that holds.

---

# The state of play

The discovery phase is preparation under the weight of everything that came before, and the central task is assembling the accumulated decisions into a coherent narrative. The artifacts are the team's own arc, the data-and-services thesis from Week 1, the platform and sourcing choices, the data strategy, the AI bet, the breach handling, the data-rights resolution, and the infrastructure decision, and the work is to make them into one story. For a team that played coherently this is straightforward, because the decisions already form a through-line and the narrative writes itself, and for a team that zigzagged it's nearly impossible, because the decisions contradict each other and no amount of slide design hides that the strategy never had a spine.

The board's state is the other half of the terrain, and reading it correctly is half the preparation. The factions are known, the heritage bloc and the transformation bloc and the swing, but the mood underneath them is the accumulated state, and a team that reads only the factions without reading what the arc did to the board misjudges the room. The business case has to translate the strategy into the board's language, risk and return and the financial logic, which is Reinhardt's lens, and a team that converted him has a financial ally inside the room while a team that didn't faces his challenge from the seat that matters most on the numbers. And the ask has to be sized and framed, ambitious enough to match the strategy and grounded enough that a skeptical board can grant it, because an ask that's too big gets refused and an ask that's too small concedes the prize the whole arc was for. A team that grasps that the meeting is the reckoning of its arc prepares to tell the truth well, while a team that treats it as a sales pitch divorced from its record finds the board remembers the record.

# The executive and board reads

Ashby is the mountain, and the read on him is precise, that his agreement may not be available but his respect is. He loves the machines and distrusts the pivot on principle, so a team that frames the transformation as a betrayal of the legacy loses him and the heritage bloc behind him, while a team that frames it as the legacy's next chapter, the company's craftsmanship extended into a new form, has a chance to win the respect that matters even without the agreement. Sokolski is the live attack, the director who will challenge the entire premise that DigitalCo should be anything but a machine company, and his hostile question is the test of whether the strategy can be defended under fire or collapses into qualifications.

The rest of the board is the accumulated state made into votes. Chen wants the bet and demands the numbers, so a team that built a real advantage and can show the return has her while a team that produced theater or a hollow spine has her doubt, and her impatience is the reason the ask has to be credible rather than aspirational. Whitfield champions the transformation and is the team's ally in the room, with the caveat that she may set expectations higher than the team can deliver, so her advocacy is useful and needs gentle calibration. Hargrove is the swing whose confidence tracks performance, and his vote was earned or lost across thirteen weeks rather than won in this meeting, which makes him the cleanest measure of whether the team actually earned the board. Calloway carries the pitch or doesn't on the strength of the Week 2 cover and the delivery since, and Reinhardt is the financial ally or the financial challenger depending on the conversion the team did or didn't earn, and the week rewards a team that walks in having earned the room rather than hoping to win it in the moment.

# The decision

The team submits the board deck and delivers the presentation, the heaviest deliverable in the sim alongside the synthesis, a board-grade deck and presentation at the graduate level judged at a near-professional standard with no scaffolding, and a deck built on the business-case and board-communication frameworks at the undergraduate level with the narrative and the ask structured by those lenses. The submission carries the strategic narrative, the coherent story of the data-and-services transformation grounded in the accumulated decisions, and a team can only tell the story its arc actually supports. It carries the business case, the risk and return and financial logic in the board's language. And it carries the ask, sized and framed, the funding and mandate and go-decision the strategy justifies and the board can grant.

The tempting moves sit in front of the team unlabeled, and the central one is the incoherence reckoning. A pitch that contradicts the team's own earlier choices, because the team optimized each week locally without a through-line, has no coherent story to tell and falls apart under the board's questioning, which is the audit doing exactly what it exists to do. Burying the board in technical jargon is the delivery trap, losing a room that doesn't speak IT. Mis-sizing the ask, too big to grant or too small to matter, is the framing trap. And folding under Sokolski's hostile question is the nerve trap, the strategy that can't be defended when challenged. The sounder path is a coherent narrative the arc actually supports, told in the board's language, with a well-sized ask defended under fire, and the difficulty of producing it is set by how the team played, because a coherent team tells the truth well while a zigzagging team can't manufacture a spine it never had. The students experience these as open, and the scoring teaches that the board meeting was always going to reveal whether the strategy was real.

The submission runs across the four dimensions as a heavy deliverable, and Coherence carries the most weight of any week in the entire sim, because this is the audit. Coherence scores whether the pitch holds together with all the prior weeks' choices, so a team that played one continuous strategy can tell a coherent story and a team that zigzagged cannot, and the contradiction is fully visible here in a way it never was before, which makes this the climax of the coherence dimension and the week where the whole arc's through-line is settled. Deliverable Quality is high because the deck is the marquee artifact, with the undergraduate bar being a well-structured framework-built deck and the graduate bar being a board-grade artifact in executive language that holds up under pressure, and this is where the tiers diverge most. The trap detection lives inside these four, with the incoherence reckoning scoring as a heavy Coherence hit, the jargon as a Deliverable and Execution hit, the mis-sized ask as a Strategic Judgment hit, and the fold under the hostile question as a Strategic Judgment and Execution hit. The accumulated-state mood is the week's distinctive feature, because the same deck delivered to a board that's lost confidence and a board that's willing to listen lands very differently, so a team's earlier performance shapes the difficulty of the ask in a way that's realistic and deliberate, and a team that earned its board across thirteen weeks plays this week on easier ground than a team that arrives having burned it, which is the relationship version of the coherence lesson.

# What propagates forward

Week 13 sets the terms of the endgame. The board's decision, to grant the ask, deny it, or signal lost confidence, feeds Week 14 directly, because a granted ask sets up the bold endgame and a denial or a confidence loss constrains it, and the board's verdict becomes part of the integrated position the synthesis has to reckon with. The ask outcome shapes the tier the team can reach, with a board that commits funding and mandate and a go-decision pointing toward the top outcome and a board that refuses pulling the ceiling down.

The coherence and relationship threads resolve here. The whole-sim coherence is fully reckoned at the audit, so the trajectory that ran from the Week 1 anchor through every week's choice reaches its settlement, and the Week 14 synthesis becomes the final integration of an arc whose coherence is now known rather than accumulating. The relationship threads resolve too, with Calloway's cover, Reinhardt's conversion, and Hargrove's swing all paying off or coming up empty in the room, and the board confidence the team carries out of this meeting becoming the mood the endgame inherits. If the week is run as live presentations to a faculty or peer panel, the ranking here is a major competitive input, the showcase moment where the field's coherence is judged in public.

# MIS versus MISX in Week 13

The week is identical for both courses, and the tiers diverge here more than anywhere else in the sim. MISX teams get the frameworks named, business-case construction and board communication and strategic narrative and the CFO's lens offered as the lenses, the deck structured by those frameworks, the ask framing guided, the hostile question flagged so the undergraduates can prepare for it, and the board's factions and mood drawn more clearly, so the undergraduate deck is graded on framework application and structure. MIS teams get the deck unscaffolded, a board-grade artifact with no template, the ask framing open, the hostile question arriving as a live surprise, and the board's mood read unaided, so the graduate deck is graded on analytical depth and coherence and persuasiveness under pressure at a near-professional standard, which is the closest the academic tier comes to the executive reality of presenting to a board that owes you nothing. The advisors say the same things in the same voices to both, and the difference is only in how much the wrapper helps the team turn its arc into a story the board will buy.
