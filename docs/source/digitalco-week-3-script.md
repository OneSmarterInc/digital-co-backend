# DigitalCo Week 3: The Reckoning
## The Failing Project, the Sunk-Cost Trap, and the First Gate

Week 3 follows the five-beat shape the earlier weeks established, so this script applies the pattern rather than re-explaining it. What makes Week 3 distinct is that it's the first crisis the CIO has to manage rather than diagnose, and the first place the governance built in Week 2 gets tested under real pressure. The S/4HANA migration that Tom Bryce launched hits the wall, and the team faces the sunk-cost reckoning, the hardest and least flashy decision in the early arc, alongside a trap that looks exactly like rescue and a gate that can quietly cap the whole endgame.

---

# The cold open

The crisis arrives over a weekend. A long-planned cutover attempt fails, the data migration throws errors, the new core breaks against the legacy IBM i it was supposed to sit beside, and the business can't process orders for the better part of a day. When the dust settles and someone finally takes a hard look at the program, the real state comes into view, the date has slipped again, the spend has crossed forty million against an original budget near twenty-five, and the reason every cutover breaks is the one Marcus flagged in Week 1, that the dependencies between S/4 and the legacy core were never fully mapped. The failed cutover is the flashpoint, and it lands at the worst possible moment, with the board meeting days away.

The pressure converges from every direction at once. Reinhardt is alarmed in the specific way of a man watching his worst prediction come true, because this is the overrun he's been losing sleep over since Week 1, and he wants the bleeding stopped now. The business is in revolt, because the migration keeps disrupting operations and delivering nothing, and Petrillo wants to know when IT will stop breaking her ability to ship product. Chen wants accountability for the program her firm funded, and Calloway watches to see how the new CIO handles a crisis that isn't theirs but is now entirely their responsibility. The task lands hard, to decide the fate of the migration, rescue it, restructure it, descope it, or kill it, and to communicate that decision to a board and a business that have run out of patience.

---

# The state of play

The discovery phase puts the real condition of the program in front of the team, and the artifacts are unforgiving. The true budget and schedule are there to examine, the forty million and the repeatedly slipped dates, alongside the cutover post-mortem that shows what actually broke and why. The artifact that matters most is the absence of one, the dependency map that was never built, because that gap is the root cause, the reason the cutover fails every time and the reason nobody can say with confidence what finishing the migration would actually take. A team that grasps the missing dependency map understands the decision it's facing, while a team that reads this as simply a behind-schedule project reaches for the wrong answer.

The options are real and they're genuinely different. Rescuing means pushing the migration through to completion, which sounds decisive but means finishing a system nobody fully understands on top of dependencies nobody mapped, at a cost and risk no one can bound. Restructuring means stopping, mapping the dependencies properly, and re-planning with real stage-gates, slower and more honest. Descoping means cutting the migration back to what's actually achievable and stabilizing a smaller, real version of it. Killing means abandoning S/4, stabilizing on the IBM i that's still running the business anyway, and rethinking the core later from a position of knowledge rather than panic. And sitting alongside the options is the integrator's pitch, which is where the trap lives.

The systems integrator offers a lifeline. They say they can get the cutover done fast with a proprietary accelerator and a workaround, for more money, and it looks like salvation, finish the thing, stop the pain, save face in front of the board. What the pitch actually is, underneath the relief it offers, is a deferral that papers over the dependency problem rather than solving it, deepens the company's dependence on the integrator and its proprietary tooling, and pushes the real architectural reckoning down the road to where it surfaces again in the vendor crisis of Week 7 and the infrastructure decision of Week 12. The lifeline is the sunk-cost reflex wearing the costume of a solution.

---

# The executive and board reads

This is Reinhardt's crisis more than anyone's, and how the team handles him shapes the rest of the sim. He's pushing hard to kill the program or finish it cleanly and stop the spend, and he's not wrong to be angry, but he's also been waiting since Week 1 for someone to handle an IT disaster with rigor instead of optimism, so a team that brings him a forward-looking, sunk-cost-disciplined plan does more to convert him here than anywhere else in the arc, while a team that panics or spins confirms every fear he's carried. Chen wants accountability and a credible path to a return on the program her firm funded, and she's the reason the decision can't be only operational, it has to answer to the people who put the money in.

The rest of the cast presses from their corners. Petrillo speaks for the business in revolt and wants the disruption to end, which is a legitimate operational demand the team has to weigh against the temptation to keep pushing a broken cutover. Calloway stays in his watching posture, reading whether this CIO is the kind who owns a hard call or the kind who reaches for a comforting story, and the read he forms here carries into how much cover he's willing to extend later. A team that treats the board and the business as an audience to be managed honestly does better than one that treats them as a problem to be spun, because everyone in this particular room has already been burned once by exactly that.

---

# The advisors in Week 3

This is Marcus Webb's week to lead, because the decision can't be made well without understanding what's actually salvageable. He explains why the migration stalled, the unmapped coupling between the new core and the legacy IBM i, and he reads the options honestly, that descope-and-stabilize is viable and that a clean kill is survivable because the IBM i is still running the business regardless. His sharpest contribution is on the lifeline, which he sees for what it is, "you can finish the cutover their way, but you'll be finishing a system you don't understand on dependencies nobody mapped, and you'll pay for that twice." He's dry and precise, and he's the antidote to the illusion that the integrator's accelerator is a fix rather than a deferral.

Diane Brandt works the part Marcus can't, the sunk-cost discipline and the communication. She names the trap directly, that the forty million is gone whether the team finishes or not and the only live question is the best path from here, and she coaches on owning the decision with the board without destroying credibility, "they'll forgive a clear-eyed kill faster than they'll forgive a hopeful lie." Frank Delgado reads the integrator's incentive and the lock-in inside the lifeline, pressing the team to ask what the accelerator costs to walk away from later rather than only what it costs now, and weighing whether to renegotiate the integrator relationship or replace it outright. Daniel Stern brings the strategic frame and a little of his characteristic pull, asking whether finishing S/4 serves the data-and-services strategy or just the ego of completion, "is this the foundation of the strategy or a monument to the last guy's mistake," with his bias tugging toward abandoning the legacy work entirely to fund the bold bet, which might be right and might be reckless depending on whether the core genuinely needs stabilizing. Renata Voss is lighter but holds the line, noting that a failed cutover leaves data in inconsistent states that are a risk surface of their own, and that the factory-floor exposure is still sitting unaddressed while everyone's consumed by this fire.

# The decision

The team submits the migration decision and the plan to carry it out, with the deliverable scaling to the tier, an executive memo at the graduate level that reasons to the recovery or kill decision and defends it, and a structured analysis at the undergraduate level that applies the named lenses, project governance, the sunk-cost fallacy, and stage-gating, to reach the same call. Four things have to be in it. The first is the fate of the migration, a forward-looking choice among rescue, restructure, descope, and kill, judged on the quality of the reasoning from here rather than the size of the investment behind. The second is a credible plan to execute that choice, the scope and stabilization path if descoping, the dependency-mapped re-plan with gates if restructuring, the stabilization on the IBM i and the path forward for the core if killing. The third is the integrator decision, renegotiate, replace, or take the accelerator, with eyes open about lock-in. The fourth is the communication, how to own this with the board, the business, and Reinhardt.

The tempting moves are laid out unlabeled, and the central one is the sunk-cost trap. Pushing the migration through to completion because the company has already spent forty million and come too far feels like resolve and lands as throwing good money after bad, finishing a system nobody understands at an unbounded cost. The integrator's lifeline is the same instinct made comfortable, relief now in exchange for deeper lock-in and a deferred reckoning, and a team that grabs it pays in Weeks 7 and 12. The communication has its own trap, because blame-shifting onto Bryce or the integrator is the natural reflex and the board sees straight through it, where transparent ownership of the real state and a clear plan builds credibility even though it's painful to deliver. The sounder path is the forward-looking decision, most often descope-and-stabilize or a properly restructured re-plan and sometimes an outright kill, paired with a clean integrator move that avoids the lifeline's lock-in and a transparent accounting to the board. The students experience all of this as open, and the scoring teaches the difference between reasoning forward and rationalizing backward.

# Scoring Week 3 and the first gate

The submission runs across the four dimensions, and this week leans on Strategic Judgment and the sunk-cost reasoning inside it. Strategic Judgment rewards a forward-looking decision and a credible recovery or kill plan, and the correct read on whether to finish, descope, restructure, or kill given the unmapped dependencies, and it penalizes the push-through that rationalizes backward and the grab for the lifeline. Execution and Consequence Management scores the crisis handling, the management of the business disruption and the integrator relationship and the board and Reinhardt, and the transparency of the communication, and it credits a team that uses the governance it built in Week 2 to structure the decision and bring the factions in legitimately rather than improvising. Coherence, now in its second week of biting, scores whether the migration decision fits the Week 1 thesis and the Week 2 alignment, so a team that committed to the data-and-services direction and then pours everything into finishing a legacy migration at the expense of the strategy takes a hit for allocating against its own plan. Deliverable Quality rewards clean application of the project-governance and sunk-cost frameworks at the undergraduate tier and analytical depth on the recovery reasoning at the graduate tier.

Week 3 is where the first gate appears, and it works at the scenario level rather than the rubric level, which is the distinction that keeps the two layers clean. A team that throws money at the failing project, whether by pushing through or by buying the lifeline, without a sound plan, doesn't merely lose points, it damages the budget envelope and the board's confidence in ways that constrain everything ahead, less money for the data and AI bets in Weeks 4, 9, and 12, and less board trust to spend on the Week 13 ask. That damage caps the endgame ceiling, so a team can recover a clean Week 3 score on later deliverables and still find it can't reach the top tier because it spent the money and the trust it would have needed. The first benchmark after Week 4 is where this becomes visible, the moment the field sees who handled the inheritance and who quietly mortgaged the rest of the game to avoid admitting a loss.

---

# What propagates forward

Week 3 sets the financial and reputational conditions for the back half of the sim. A sound decision preserves the resources the data-and-services bet will need, while a sunk-cost push-through or a bought lifeline burns budget the team will be desperate for in Weeks 9 and 12. Transparent ownership builds the board and Reinhardt trust that pays off at the Week 13 board pitch, while blame-shifting or a panicked call damages it and feeds the gate. The integrator decision carries the longest tail, because grabbing the lifeline deepens a lock-in that detonates in the Week 7 vendor crisis and again in the Week 12 infrastructure reckoning, where a clean handling here would have left the team with options it now lacks.

The relationships move too. A disciplined, transparent migration decision is the single largest step toward converting Reinhardt from a brake into an ally, since this is the crisis he's been waiting to see handled with rigor, and a bad call confirms his worst read and keeps him hostile. The coherence trajectory continues to compound, with a strategy-consistent resource decision reinforcing the through-line and a sunk-cost detour starting the kind of zigzag that surfaces at the coherence audit. And the first benchmark now sits one week out, the reveal that will show the whole field who arrived at Week 4 with budget and credibility intact and who arrived having already spent both.

# MIS versus MISX in Week 3

The week is identical for both courses, and only the scaffolding forks. MISX teams get the frameworks named, the sunk-cost fallacy and project governance and stage-gating offered as the lenses, the four options drawn clearly as distinct paths, and the integrator's lifeline flagged as a tradeoff with a cost rather than a pure rescue, so the undergraduates learn to recognize sunk-cost reasoning with the trap marked. MIS teams get the sunk-cost pull unflagged, so the students have to resist the backward-looking rationalization themselves, and the lifeline presented as the attractive option it appears to be with the lock-in unmarked, and the decision framed open-ended, so the graduate students do the forward-looking reasoning and the crisis communication the way the job demands. The advisors say the same things in the same voices to both, and the difference is only in how much the wrapper helps the team see that the comfortable choice is the costly one.
