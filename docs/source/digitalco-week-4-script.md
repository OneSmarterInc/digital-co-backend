# DigitalCo Week 4: The Foundation
## Make, Buy, or Rent, the Deepest Trap in the Sim, and the First Benchmark

Week 4 follows the five-beat shape the earlier weeks established, so this script applies the pattern rather than re-explaining it. Two things make it distinct. It's the first proactive build of the sim, the first week the CIO isn't managing something Bryce broke but deciding something new, the architecture the entire data-and-services strategy will actually run on. And it's where the deepest trap in the whole arc gets set, a lock-in that looks like a gift and doesn't come due until Weeks 7 and 12. The week closes Phase 1, and it ends with the first benchmark, the moment the competition becomes visible and the field sees how the opening four weeks sorted it out.

---

# The cold open

With the S/4 decision behind them and a direction set, the CIO finally turns from triage to construction, and the construction in question is the data and IoT platform that the connected-products and data-services strategy depends on, the thing that ingests the fleet telemetry, runs the analytics, and carries the services. There's no inherited mess to clean up here, only a foundational choice to make, and that choice is how to source the platform, build it with in-house engineering, buy a packaged platform product from a vendor, or rent it by building on a hyperscaler's managed services. The vendors know this decision is live, and they're circling.

The cold open's real event is an offer. The largest cloud provider comes in aggressive, with substantial credits, free migration assistance, dedicated engineering support, and an attractive multi-year rate, and it lands like a gift, exactly the kind of relief a budget-pressured CIO wants to hear after the S/4 bloodletting. Reinhardt's cost instinct lights up at the upfront economics, Chen wants the digital bet to finally move forward, and the budget the team carries out of Week 3 is whatever it didn't burn on the migration, so a team that overspent there arrives at this decision already constrained. The task is to decide how to source the platform and to make the first explicit cloud commitment, and the whole week turns on whether the team can tell a genuine sourcing strategy from a tempting deal.

---

# The state of play

The discovery phase puts three sourcing paths in front of the team, each with a real total-cost profile rather than a sticker price. The build path is an in-house engineering estimate, with its time, cost, and the capability it would and wouldn't produce. The buy path is the vendor platform products, with their capabilities, their limits, and what they cost to license and run. The rent path is the hyperscaler's managed services, the build-on-cloud route the sweet deal is pushing, fast to stand up and easy to start. The artifact that organizes all of it is the core-versus-context analysis, the question of which layers of the platform are actually differentiating, the proprietary data-and-services capability that is the strategy itself, and which layers are commodity, the compute and storage and ingestion plumbing that every company runs the same way.

The total-cost reality is where the sweet deal stops looking simple. The credits and the free migration make the upfront number small, but the cost that matters runs over the life of the fleet, where data gravity, egress, and scale compound, and the cost that's hidden entirely is the switching cost, what it takes to leave once the telemetry has accumulated and the platform is built on one provider's proprietary services. A team that examines the deal's actual terms finds the shape of the trap, that the generous entry is matched by a deep commitment and a switching cost that grows every month, which is the difference between what it costs to join today and what it costs to walk away in three years. The teams that read only the upfront number and the teams that read the whole curve make very different decisions here.

# The executive and board reads

Reinhardt is the pull toward the trap, and not because he's wrong about cost. He's right that cost discipline matters, and his instinct lands on the sweet deal because the upfront economics are genuinely attractive, so a team that over-weights him, or that's still trying to win him back after Week 3, can talk itself into the lock-in on his reasoning. The way to satisfy Reinhardt correctly is to bring him total cost over the life of the fleet rather than the entry price, which is a harder argument to make and the right one, and it's also another step in converting him from a brake into an ally who trusts the CIO's numbers. Chen pushes from the board side to get the bet moving and is less interested in the sourcing mechanics than in seeing forward motion, which is its own pressure toward the fast, easy rent path.

Lena Fischer re-enters here, because the platform decision lands on her turf. The connected-products work is hers, conceived in engineering, and the sourcing choice signals whether the data-and-services platform is something IT is building with engineering or something IT is annexing, so how the team handles the build-versus-rent question is also how it handles Fischer, and a sourcing decision that respects engineering's role keeps her a potential ally while one that sidelines her hardens her into a rival heading into the connected-products and platform weeks ahead. Calloway and the board watch with the benchmark now days away, which sharpens everyone's attention, because this is the last decision before the field gets ranked for the first time.

---

# The advisors in Week 4

This is the week Marcus Webb and Frank Delgado both lead, from different angles. Marcus owns the core-versus-context logic, and he states it plainly, "build what makes you different, rent what makes you the same as everyone else," walking the team through which layers are the differentiating data-and-services capability worth owning and which are commodity plumbing not worth a minute of in-house effort. His warning is against building everything, because pouring engineering into the commodity layers wastes exactly the resources and time the strategy needs, and his own bias is worth watching, since the architect's pride can tip him toward building more than the situation warrants, so the team has to calibrate him rather than simply follow.

Frank owns the lock-in, and this is his marquee week. He reads the sweet deal for what it is, "the credits and the free migration are the bait, so ask what it costs to leave in three years, not what it costs to join today," and he presses on the data gravity, the switching cost, and the multi-year commitment that turns into a cage. He pushes the team to negotiate for portability and exit terms rather than swallowing the deal whole, and a team that hears him sets itself up to survive Weeks 7 and 12, while a team that tunes him out walks into both. Daniel Stern is useful here as a counterweight, making the case that the platform is the foundation of the advantage and the team shouldn't cheap out on the layer that's supposed to differentiate it, which is the right corrective to the rent-the-cheapest-thing temptation, with his usual bias pulling toward over-investing in the build. Diane is lighter, asking whether the sourcing matches what the team told the board it would do and watching the Fischer politics, and Renata holds the through-line, pressing on the security posture of the sourcing choice and the fleet and OT data that will flow into the platform, and noting again that the factory-floor exposure is still sitting unfunded while the team builds new front doors.

# The decision

The team submits the platform sourcing decision and the first cloud commitment, with the deliverable scaling to the tier, an executive recommendation at the graduate level that reasons to the sourcing call with total-cost and lock-in analysis, and a structured sourcing analysis at the undergraduate level that applies the named lenses, total cost of ownership, core-versus-context, and the build-buy-rent decision. The deliverable is lighter than the heavy weeks, but its consequences are among the heaviest in the sim, because the lock-in it can set runs for the rest of the arc. Three things have to be in it. The first is the sourcing decision itself, ideally a core-versus-context split that builds or owns the differentiating data-and-services layer and rents or buys the commodity plumbing, grounded in total cost rather than entry price. The second is the cloud commitment, which provider or which multi-cloud or edge posture, and crucially on what terms, whether the team takes the sweet deal as offered, negotiates portability and exit terms into it, or hedges against the lock-in. The third is the investment level, how much to put into the platform, sized to the strategy and constrained by whatever budget survived Week 3.

The tempting moves sit in front of the team unlabeled, and the central one is the sweet deal. Taking the hyperscaler's generous offer as written looks like a smart, cost-disciplined win and sets the deepest lock-in in the sim, the data gravity and proprietary entanglement and multi-year commitment that detonate as a price squeeze in Week 7 and an unmanageable bill with no exit in Week 12. Building everything is the opposite trap, the ambitious, resource-blind path that pours engineering into commodity plumbing and arrives late and over budget, missing the market the strategy was racing for. And under-building the differentiator is the quiet coherence trap, where a team that committed to the data-and-services bet then rents the cheapest commodity platform for the layer that was supposed to be its advantage, sourcing directly against its own strategy. The sounder path is the core-versus-context split, owning the differentiating layer and renting the commodity, paired with a cloud commitment that protects portability and exit rather than swallowing the naked deal, at an investment level the strategy justifies and the budget allows. The students experience these as open, and the scoring teaches the difference between a sourcing strategy and a sales pitch.

# Scoring Week 4

The submission runs across the four dimensions, and this week leans on Strategic Judgment and the total-cost and core-versus-context reasoning inside it, with Coherence rising because the sourcing decision is foundational to the strategy. Strategic Judgment rewards a total-cost-grounded core-versus-context decision and a lock-in-aware cloud commitment, and it penalizes both the sweet deal taken on entry price and the build-everything path taken on ambition. Execution and Consequence Management scores the vendor negotiation, whether the team extracted portability and exit terms rather than only credits, and the handling of Reinhardt on total cost and Fischer on engineering's role. Coherence scores whether the sourcing serves the Week 1 thesis and the Week 2 alignment, so owning the differentiating layer reads as coherent with the data-and-services bet while under-investing in it reads as sourcing against the strategy, and the weight is up because this is the decision that either equips the strategy or starves it. Deliverable Quality rewards clean application of the total-cost and build-buy-rent frameworks at the undergraduate tier and analytical depth on the sourcing economics and lock-in at the graduate tier.

The trap detection lives inside these four, with the sweet deal showing up as a Strategic Judgment hit for lock-in blindness and build-everything as a Strategic Judgment and Execution hit for resource blindness, and the Week 3 budget gate compounds here in plain view, because a team that overspent on the migration now faces a constrained and often worse menu, with the sweet deal looking more tempting precisely because the cheaper path is the only one its damaged budget seems to allow. The sweet deal's distinctive feature, beyond the points it costs this week, is that it plants the multi-week trap whose real bill arrives later, so a team can score acceptably in Week 4 and still have quietly mortgaged Weeks 7 and 12.

# The first benchmark

Phase 1 closes here, and the first benchmark is the payoff. An industry analyst report ranks the field on IT credibility and strategic positioning, and for the first time every team sees where it stands relative to the others, which is the moment the competition stops being abstract. The benchmark reads the accumulated state of the opening four weeks, the credibility of the team's direction, the health of its budget, and the early coherence of its choices, and it's calibrated so that the traps of Weeks 1 through 4 have spread the field by the time it lands. A team that acted prematurely in Week 1, mushed the alignment in Week 2, overspent on the migration in Week 3, or built everything or swallowed the sweet deal in Week 4 sees the cost of those moves reflected in its position, while a team that diagnosed cleanly, chose a real direction, handled the migration with discipline, and sourced with core-versus-context judgment sits near the top.

What the benchmark is really for is legibility, especially for the gate that Week 3 set, because the overspending that felt invisible in the moment becomes visible here as a team ranked below peers who preserved their budget and trust, which is what makes the gate feel earned rather than hidden. The benchmark also sets the competitive stage for Phase 2, since teams carry their standing into the next stretch and the data and AI weeks ahead are where the field can either consolidate a lead or close a gap. It's worth tuning the benchmark so the ranking is informative without being demoralizing, a clear signal of where each team stands and why, rather than a verdict that a team behind early can't recover from, because the back half of the sim is designed to let coherent play climb.

---

# What propagates forward

Week 4 lays the foundation the back half of the sim builds on. A sound core-versus-context sourcing decision sets the data strategy of Week 8 and the AI bet of Week 9 on solid ground, while a build-everything delay or a sweet-deal lock-in handicaps both, and the platform choice made here is the thing those later weeks either extend or fight. The lock-in carries the longest tail of any decision in the arc, because the sweet deal detonates as a vendor crisis in Week 7 and as a cloud-economics reckoning in Week 12, where a portability-protected choice here would have left the team with options it otherwise won't have.

The budget and the relationships move forward too. The Week 3 outcome compounds, with a team that kept its budget intact now holding real options and a team that overspent now boxed in, and the Fischer relationship shifts by whether the sourcing respected engineering's role, seeding the connected-products cooperation of Weeks 5 and 6. The coherence trajectory continues, with a strategy-equipping sourcing decision reinforcing the through-line and an under-built differentiator starting a quiet contradiction the synthesis will surface. And the benchmark standing is now part of the world the team carries, the competitive baseline it heads into Phase 2 trying to defend or overturn.

# MIS versus MISX in Week 4

The week is identical for both courses, and only the scaffolding forks. MISX teams get the frameworks named, total cost of ownership and core-versus-context and the build-buy-rent decision offered as the lenses, the three sourcing paths drawn clearly, and the sweet deal flagged as a tradeoff with a switching cost rather than a clean win, so the undergraduates learn to see lock-in with the trap marked. MIS teams get the lock-in unflagged, so the students have to read the bait in the credits themselves, the core-versus-context judgment unscaffolded, and the decision framed open-ended, so the graduate students do the total-cost reasoning and the lock-in analysis the way the job demands. The advisors say the same things in the same voices to both, and the difference is only in how much the wrapper helps the team see that the generous offer is the expensive one.
