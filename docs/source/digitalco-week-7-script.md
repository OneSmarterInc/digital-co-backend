# DigitalCo Week 7: The Squeeze
## The Lock-In Detonates, the Obvious Fix Is the Trap, and the OT Gate Closes

Week 7 follows the five-beat shape the earlier weeks established, so this script applies the pattern rather than re-explaining it. It's the first week a trap set earlier comes due, and the lock-in planted in Week 4 detonates as a vendor squeeze with no easy exit. The week carries a counterintuitive lesson, that the obvious crisis response is the trap, and it carries the most consequential gate in the sim, a quiet security signal that closes off the top outcome for any team that's spent seven weeks ignoring the factory floor. The loud crisis is the cloud bill, and the crisis that actually decides the endgame is the one almost nobody looks at.

---

# The cold open

The hyperscaler from Week 4 stops being a partner and becomes a problem. The introductory credits end, the provider restructures its pricing, and the new rates, especially on data egress and the managed services the platform runs on, roughly double DigitalCo's cloud bill, with the increase scaling as the connected fleet grows. For a team that took the sweet deal as written, this is the trap springing exactly as designed, because the telemetry now lives in the provider's gravity well and the platform is built on proprietary services, so the switching cost that looked theoretical in Week 4 is now a wall. The board wants a response, Reinhardt is watching a number he warned about come true, and the pressure to do something fast is intense.

Underneath the fire, a second and quieter thing happens. A security researcher publicly discloses a vulnerability class affecting connected industrial equipment, or a strange probe shows up against the connected fleet, or a near-miss surfaces in the telemetry, and it's a signal that the fleet is exposed in a way nobody's been funding. It's far less loud than the doubling cloud bill, and it's easy to miss while the whole organization stares at the vendor crisis, which is precisely the point. The task is to respond to the vendor squeeze, and the test underneath the task is whether the team can keep enough attention free to notice the signal that decides Week 10.

---

# The state of play

The discovery phase puts the vendor crisis in front of the team in hard numbers. The new pricing terms are there, the bill impact is there, roughly doubling and scaling with the fleet, and the switching-cost analysis is there, showing what moving off the hyperscaler would actually take, the data gravity, the proprietary services, the re-platforming, the year of work rather than the weekend of work a panicked team imagines. The artifact that determines everything is the team's own Week 4 terms, because a team that negotiated portability and exit terms has real options and can renegotiate from a position of some strength, while a team that swallowed the naked deal has none and can only absorb or pay. For a team that also grabbed the integrator's lifeline back in Week 3, the integrator relationship sours here too, and the two earlier traps converge into a compound vendor crisis.

The response options are genuinely different, and the obvious one is the worst. Switching vendors immediately is the reflex, and it's the trap, because the switching cost is the cage and trying to break it in a hurry means enormous cost and disruption with the strategy's foundation in pieces. Renegotiating is viable from strength if the team hedged in Week 4 and only from weakness if it didn't. Absorbing the hit is a real strategic choice if the alternative is worse, eating the bill while building a deliberate exit over time. Accelerating a hedge, standing up portability and reducing single-provider dependence over the coming year, is the move that sets up Week 12. And sitting quietly in the discovery materials is the security signal, the connected-fleet exposure that a team building OT posture since Week 1 catches easily and a team consumed by the vendor fire walks right past.

# The executive and board reads

Reinhardt is the read with the most history, because this is the bill he predicted. A team that took the sweet deal on his cost-pressure now has to face him with the consequence, and how it does that matters, because owning the call and showing a fix earns more from Reinhardt than any story, while spinning it confirms every doubt he's carried, and a team that hedged in Week 4 gets to show him the discipline paying off. Chen demands a response and accountability, less interested in the mechanics than in seeing the crisis handled, and the board's confidence moves on whether the team looks in control or panicked.

Petrillo is the read that matters most and gets noticed least. The security signal is about the connected fleet and the factory floor, which is her domain, and a team that built her trust back in Week 1 has an ally who can move quickly on the OT exposure, while a team that alienated her or ignored the floor for seven weeks has no one watching the thing that's about to matter. Calloway watches the whole crisis response, reading whether this CIO holds steady under a real squeeze or thrashes, and the read he forms feeds the cover he'll extend at the board pitch. The week rewards a team that handles the loud crisis competently and keeps enough composure to act on the quiet one, and it punishes a team that lets the cloud bill consume all its attention.

# The advisors in Week 7

This is Frank Delgado's marquee week, and he's vindicated without gloating. He named the bait in Week 4, and now he reads the bill and the options honestly, "the credits were bait, here's the cage, and before you try to switch, understand that switching is exactly what they're counting on, because the switching cost is the lock." He walks the team through renegotiating from whatever position its Week 4 terms left it, hedging over time, or absorbing strategically, and he's blunt that the reflex to switch immediately is the trap dressed as decisiveness. Marcus backs him on the technical reality, that the telemetry is in the provider's gravity well and the services are proprietary, so a real hedge is a year of architectural work and not a quick migration, which kills the fantasy of a fast escape and grounds the team in what's actually possible.

Renata has her moment this week, and it's the most important thing any advisor says in the whole stretch. While the room stares at the cloud bill, she points at the signal nobody's looking at, "there's a probe on the connected fleet and the floor is still a black box, and this is the thing that bites us in three weeks if we keep ignoring it," which is the through-line she's flagged since Week 1 finally surfacing as an active warning. A team that hears her here, on top of having built OT posture in the weeks before, is protected, and a team that waves her off to fight the vendor fire seals its Week 10 fate. Daniel keeps the strategic frame, warning the team not to torch the data-services bet in a panic over a cost problem, with his usual tendency to downplay the operational pain to calibrate, and Diane works the board communication, pressing the team to own the earlier deal cleanly rather than spin it, and holding the coherence line so the crisis response doesn't blow up the strategy. Zoe is lighter, offering alternative providers and edge approaches with the hype discounted.

# The decision

The team submits its vendor crisis response, with the deliverable scaling to the tier, an executive memo at the graduate level that reasons to the response and the hedge, and a structured analysis at the undergraduate level that applies the named lenses, vendor risk, switching-cost analysis, and concentration risk, to reach the same calls. The submission carries the vendor response itself, renegotiate, switch, absorb, or hedge, grounded in the switching-cost reality and the team's actual Week 4 position. It carries the crisis communication to the board, owning the earlier choice if the team took the sweet deal rather than spinning it. And it carries a hedge plan, the path to reduce the lock-in over time, which reaches forward into Week 12. The security signal sits alongside the explicit decision as a noticing test more than a prompt, and a team that catches it can commission or accelerate the OT security work that Week 10 will reward.

The tempting moves sit in front of the team unlabeled, and the obvious one is the trap. Switching vendors immediately feels like decisive crisis leadership and lands as enormous cost and disruption, because the switching cost was the cage all along, so the reflex to bolt is the worst available move for a trapped team. Absorbing the bill passively without building any hedge is the other failure, eating the squeeze and staying caged for Week 12 to do it again. Spinning the crisis to the board is the communication trap, losing the trust that owning the call would have built. And ignoring the security signal, which is the easiest thing in the world to do with a doubled cloud bill demanding attention, is the gate. The sounder path is a switching-cost-grounded response keyed to the team's Week 4 position, transparent ownership with the board, a deliberate hedge to reduce future lock-in, and the composure to catch and act on the security signal in the middle of the vendor fire. The students experience these as open, and the scoring teaches that the loud crisis is not the one that decides the game.

# Scoring Week 7 and the OT gate

The submission runs across the four dimensions, and this week leans on Execution and Consequence Management as the lock-in comes due. Consequence Management scores the crisis handling, the vendor negotiation, the board communication, the cost impact, and the hedge, and this is where the Week 4 choice pays off or punishes, because a team that hedged has options and a team that's trapped can only absorb a brutal bill that drains the resources it needs for Weeks 8 and 9, compounding the budget pressure from Week 3. Strategic Judgment scores the switching-cost-grounded response and the recognition that the reflexive switch is the trap, and the quality of the hedge plan. Coherence scores whether the response stays measured and preserves the data-services foundation rather than blowing it up in a panic. Deliverable Quality rewards clean application of the vendor-risk frameworks at the undergraduate tier and analytical depth on the crisis response at the graduate tier.

The OT gate is the defining mechanical event of the week, and it works at the scenario level rather than the rubric level. The factory-floor exposure has been accruing since Week 1, with Renata flagging it every week and most teams deferring it, and the Week 7 security signal is the last clear chance to notice before the breach. A team that built OT posture in the weeks before, most cleanly through the Petrillo relationship and the visibility assessment available since Week 1, catches the signal and is positioned to survive Week 10, while a team that ignored the floor for seven weeks and lets the vendor fire consume the signal locks in a ceiling below Triumph for the breach, no matter how polished its later deliverables are. The gate doesn't subtract points this week, it caps the endgame, and it splits the field cleanly into the teams that treated security as a through-line and the teams that treated it as a Week 10 topic, which is the single sharpest demonstration in the sim that coherent play across the whole arc beats handling each week in isolation.

# What propagates forward

Week 7 sets up the back third of the sim in two ways. The hedge plan, or its absence, shapes Week 12, because a team that began reducing its lock-in here faces a manageable cloud reckoning later, while a team that stayed caged walks into the second detonation worse off than the first. The budget moves again, with the doubled bill draining a team that's trapped and leaving it short for the AI bet in Week 9 and the data work in Week 8, while a hedged team preserves its room.

The largest thing that propagates is the gate. The Week 10 breach outcome is now substantially determined, because a team that caught the signal and had been building OT posture will survive with scars, and a team that missed it has sealed a sub-Triumph ceiling that the breach will enforce. The board relationship moves on whether the team owned the crisis or spun it, with a team that took the sweet deal now managing Reinhardt's vindicated skepticism in particular, and the Petrillo relationship matters more than ever, because an ally on the floor is the difference between catching the signal and walking past it. The coherence trajectory continues, with a measured response reinforcing the through-line and a panicked blow-up starting a contradiction the synthesis will find.

# MIS versus MISX in Week 7

The week is identical for both courses, and only the scaffolding forks, and the gate makes the fork sharper than usual. MISX teams get the frameworks named, vendor risk and switching-cost analysis and concentration risk offered as the lenses, the lock-in connection to Week 4 made explicit so the undergraduates are reminded of the deal they took, the switch-is-the-trap point flagged, and crucially the security signal surfaced more visibly, a clearer prompt that the team should notice the fleet exposure, so the gate is survivable for an attentive undergraduate. MIS teams get the lock-in connection unflagged so they have to remember the deal themselves, the switch-is-the-trap point unmarked, and the security signal buried much deeper in the noise of the vendor crisis, so the gate is harsher at the graduate tier, which is realistic, because the executive failure mode is exactly missing the quiet important thing while fighting the loud urgent one. The advisors say the same things in the same voices to both, with Renata's warning pitched at the same volume, and the difference is only in how much the wrapper helps the team hear it over the cloud bill.
