# DigitalCo: The Deliverable Rubrics
## Paired Undergraduate and Graduate Grading Sheets for the Heavy-Deliverable Weeks

These rubrics turn the two-tier dial into something a faculty member can actually grade with. They cover the four weeks that carry the heaviest deliverables, Week 1's current-state assessment, Week 8's data and analytics strategy, Week 13's board presentation, and Week 14's integrative synthesis, and they establish the pattern the remaining ten weeks inherit at lighter weight. The central rule holds throughout, that the undergraduate and graduate versions are never graded on different things, only on the same four dimensions at different weights and a different bar.

---

# The shared architecture

Every deliverable is scored on the same four dimensions. Strategic Judgment asks whether the decision was sound given what was knowable that week. Execution and Consequence Management asks whether the team handled the stakeholders and the second-order effects. Coherence asks whether the deliverable aligns with the team's prior choices and its declared strategy. Deliverable Quality asks how well the artifact itself is built, and this is the dimension that carries most of the difference between the tiers.

Each dimension is graded in three bands. Strong means the work meets the bar for its tier with little to fault, Adequate means it's competent but with real gaps, and Weak means it misses the mark or falls into a trap. A simple convention turns bands into points, with Strong earning close to a dimension's full weight, Adequate earning roughly two-thirds, and Weak earning a third or less, and the per-week weights below all sum to a hundred so a grader can read a week's score straight off the four bands. The numbers are a sensible default and meant to be tuned to your own point scheme.

The tiers differ in two ways that run underneath every rubric. The weighting differs, with the undergraduate tier tilting weight toward Deliverable Quality and the framework application inside it, and the graduate tier tilting toward Strategic Judgment under ambiguity and Coherence, because the undergraduate is being assessed on applying the right lens well while the graduate is being assessed on judgment with no lens handed over. The bar differs too, with the same band meaning different things, so a Strong graduate memo reasons to its conclusions at a near-professional standard with no framework scaffolding, while a Strong undergraduate assessment selects and applies the named frameworks correctly and organizes the work around them.

One boundary keeps the rubrics clean. They grade the deliverable, and they feed the accumulating four-dimension totals across the sim, but they don't enforce the gates. The through-line gates, the security and OT exposure most of all, operate at the scenario level and cap the endgame tier regardless of how polished any single deliverable was, so a team can write an excellent Week 9 memo and still hit the OT ceiling in Week 10. The rubric measures the artifact, the scenario measures the consequences, and the endgame combines the accumulated dimension scores with the gate status.

# Week 1 — The current-state assessment

The deliverable is the inherited-environment diagnosis plus priorities, a strategy statement, and a posture on early action. The undergraduate version is a structured assessment that names and applies the relevant lenses, the current-state and capability-maturity read, the stakeholder analysis, and the root-cause-versus-symptom distinction, with the framework scaffolding visible in the work. The graduate version is an executive memo written to Calloway that diagnoses, prioritizes, and commits to a thesis while reasoning to its conclusions with no lenses labeled.

Strategic Judgment here is mostly about root cause and restraint. Strong work reaches the systemic diagnosis, that the real problem is a stalled and ungoverned digital bet sitting on a broken data foundation and an invisible factory floor with an IT organization that's lost the business's trust, and it explicitly resists the premature moves, declining to kill connected-products or commit to S/4 blind, and it reads the executives' self-interest rather than anchoring on the most persuasive voice. Weak work mistakes the late migration for the problem, falls for one of the trap moves, or adopts Ferraro's channel-protective read as its own. At the graduate tier this is reached unaided, and at the undergraduate tier the root-cause framework is the visible vehicle that gets the student there.

Execution and Consequence Management scores the early stakeholder handling and the sequencing. Strong work lays out a credible thirty-sixty-ninety approach and makes early moves that set up later weeks, the cleanest being the factory-floor visibility assessment with Petrillo that builds the OT relationship without betting the company, and it starts converting Reinhardt through rigor. Weak work has no real plan or burns a key relationship early. Coherence scores the strategy statement as an anchor rather than as consistency with anything prior, so Strong is a sharp, specific, defensible thesis the team can be held to for fourteen weeks, and Weak is a generic statement that could be pasted into any company's deck and commits the team to nothing. Deliverable Quality at the undergraduate tier rewards correct framework selection and a clean structure organized around the lenses, and at the graduate tier rewards a memo that reasons persuasively and reads as something a CEO would actually want to receive.

The weighting splits by tier. The undergraduate version puts the most weight on Deliverable Quality and the framework application inside it at around thirty-five, with Strategic Judgment near thirty, and Coherence and Consequence Management filling the rest at roughly twenty and fifteen. The graduate version puts the most weight on Strategic Judgment and Coherence, near thirty each because the diagnosis and the anchor matter enormously this week, with Deliverable Quality and Consequence Management splitting the remainder.

# Week 8 — The data and analytics strategy

The deliverable is the data strategy, a governance model and a stated data-rights posture aimed at turning the installed-base telemetry into competitive advantage. The undergraduate version applies the named lenses, data governance, the data value chain, data-as-asset, and analytics maturity, with the frameworks visible. The graduate version is an executive strategy memo on the data-advantage thesis and the ownership politics with no scaffolding.

Strategic Judgment turns on the data-rights trap. Strong work lands a shared-value-leaning rights model that makes the data usable and builds advantage while preserving the channel relationship, navigating the ownership question without igniting a dealer war. Weak work takes one of the two losing poles, the aggressive land grab that looks like advantage but pre-detonates the Week 11 crisis, or the ducking of the ownership question entirely that leaves no advantage to show. Execution and Consequence Management scores the handling of the channel politics through Ferraro and the anticipation of the second-order effects on dealers and customers, and it credits a team that builds the data foundation the Week 9 AI bet will depend on. Coherence matters more here than almost anywhere, because this is the keystone of the data-and-services spine, so a data strategy that contradicts the Week 1 thesis or the choices since takes a heavy hit, and one that visibly extends them scores well. Deliverable Quality rewards correct application of the data-governance frameworks at the undergraduate tier and analytical depth on the advantage-and-ownership tradeoffs at the graduate tier.

The weighting reflects the keystone status. The undergraduate version weights Deliverable Quality and Strategic Judgment most heavily at around thirty each, with Coherence rising to roughly twenty-five because this is a spine week and Consequence Management filling the rest. The graduate version weights Coherence and Strategic Judgment most heavily, near thirty-five and thirty, because keeping the spine intact under the pull of the land-grab temptation is the whole test, with Deliverable Quality and Consequence Management splitting what remains.

# Week 13 — The board presentation

The deliverable is the board deck and the presentation of the accumulated strategy with a major ask. The undergraduate version builds the deck on the business-case and board-communication frameworks, with the narrative, the risk framing, and the ask structured by those lenses. The graduate version is a board-grade deck and presentation judged at a near-professional standard, telling a coherent strategic narrative grounded in the accumulated decisions with no scaffolding.

Strategic Judgment scores whether the ask is well-sized and well-framed and whether the business case is credible, and crucially whether the strategy survives Sokolski's hostile question rather than folding into qualifications. Execution and Consequence Management scores the reading of the room, the handling of Ashby's heritage skepticism and the live attack, and the recognition that the board's receptiveness is itself a function of how the prior weeks went. Coherence carries the most weight of any week in the entire sim here, because this is the coherence audit, the moment a team that optimized week by week discovers in front of the whole board that it has no through-line to defend, while a team that played one continuous strategy can take the punch and answer it. Deliverable Quality also peaks here for both tiers since the artifact is marquee, with the undergraduate bar being a well-structured deck that follows the board-communication framework and the graduate bar being a board-grade artifact in executive language that holds up under pressure.

The weighting puts Coherence first for both tiers at around thirty-five, with Deliverable Quality high at roughly twenty-five because the deck is the showcase, and Strategic Judgment and Consequence Management splitting the remainder. The tiers diverge most here not in the weights but in the bar, since the same Strong band asks the undergraduate for a clean framework-built deck and asks the graduate for something a real board would find persuasive.

# Week 14 — The integrative synthesis

The deliverable is the final integrated position and forward strategy, and it's the endgame artifact, so it carries the heaviest weight in the course overall. The undergraduate version pulls the threads together using the course frameworks to articulate the final strategic position. The graduate version is a board-grade strategic synthesis that articulates DigitalCo's integrated position and reckons honestly with the accumulated consequences, judged on analytical depth and coherence with no scaffolding.

Strategic Judgment scores the quality of the final integrated positioning, whether it's defensible, internally consistent, and honest about where the team actually ended up. Execution and Consequence Management scores whether the synthesis reckons with the consequences the team accumulated rather than papering over the scars, and a team that owns its through-line damage reads as more credible than one that pretends the fourteen weeks went cleanly. Coherence is weighted most heavily because the synthesis is fundamentally an integration test, and it exposes plainly whether the team played one coherent strategy or fourteen disconnected weeks. Deliverable Quality rewards a framework-integrated synthesis at the undergraduate tier and a board-grade strategic document with real analytical depth at the graduate tier.

The weighting leans on Coherence and Strategic Judgment for both tiers, near thirty-five and thirty, since integration and final judgment are the point of the week, with Deliverable Quality high at around twenty-five, especially at the graduate tier where the board-grade bar is steep, and Consequence Management filling the rest. This is the week where graduate analytical depth and undergraduate framework integration diverge most sharply, so the bar gap between the tiers is wider here than anywhere else in the sim.

# How the lighter weeks inherit the pattern

The other ten weeks reuse this machinery at lower weight. Each one scores the same four dimensions in the same three bands with the same tier logic, the undergraduate version tilting toward Deliverable Quality and framework application and the graduate version toward Strategic Judgment and Coherence, and each one's deliverable is a scaled-down version of what the heavy weeks ask for, usually a shorter memo at the graduate tier or a focused framework worksheet at the undergraduate tier rather than a full strategic document. What shifts from week to week is which dimension leads, set by what the week is teaching, so the failing-project week in Week 3 leans on Strategic Judgment and the sunk-cost reasoning inside it, the vendor-crisis week in Week 7 leans on Consequence Management as the lock-in comes due, and the security-breach week in Week 10 leans on Consequence Management again while the gate does its separate work on the endgame ceiling. Once these four heavy weeks are set, writing the lighter ten is a matter of picking the lead dimension and scaling the deliverable, not designing a new rubric each time.
