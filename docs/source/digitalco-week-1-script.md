# DigitalCo Week 1: The Inheritance
## The First Buildable Module, and the Template for the Other Thirteen

Week 1 is where everything built so far converges for the first time. The inherited mess from Tom Bryce becomes something the team can examine, the six advisors come alive in conversation, the C-suite introduces itself with all its self-interest intact, and the team makes the first scored decision, the one that sets the coherence baseline for the entire fourteen-week arc. This document is detailed enough to build from, and because it's the first week it also establishes the shape every later week reuses.

The shape of a playable week is five beats. The cold open lands the situation and the task. The discovery phase lets the team examine artifacts, read the executives, and consult the advisors, all interactively, at whatever depth they choose. The decision is what the team crafts and submits, the week's deliverable plus any explicit choice. The scoring runs the submission across the four dimensions and detects the traps. The consequences change the world state, shift relationships, and propagate forward into later weeks. Week 1 walks through all five in full, and the later weeks become variations on this pattern rather than new designs.

---

# The cold open

The team arrives as DigitalCo's newly hired CIO, brought in to salvage a digital bet that's gone sideways. Ray Calloway takes the first meeting himself, which signals the stakes, and he's warm and careful in the way the roster describes. He tells the CIO that DigitalCo has to become more than an excellent machine company, that the installed base in the field is throwing off data nobody's turned into value, and that the rivals are pulling ahead on exactly that front. Then he stops short of saying what he actually wants done, because committing publicly would cost him with a board he's still managing, so he leaves the CIO with a mandate that sounds bold and specifies almost nothing. "Take thirty days, get me a real read, and come back with a direction I can take to the board."

What the CIO is walking into sits underneath that mandate. Tom Bryce, the previous CIO, sold the board on a transformation two years ago, launched a connected-products initiative and an S/4HANA migration with the private-equity firm's money, over-promised on both, and left under pressure with neither finished. The S/4 migration is stalled at a cutover nobody can complete, badly over budget and badly late. The connected-products platform ships telematics on the newer machines but monetizes almost none of it, its run cost climbing while the dealers grow uneasy about who owns the data. The data sits in a swamp, the factory floor is a black box IT can't see into, and the IT organization has lost people and lost faith.

The clock is the last thing the cold open establishes. The private-equity firm wants returns on the digital spend, the founding family is wary of what the pivot is doing to the company, and the board meets in a few weeks where Marisa Chen will expect to see the new CIO doing something. The team has thirty in-world days to assess and come back with an assessment, a set of priorities, and a strategic direction, and the pressure to arrive at that board meeting looking decisive is exactly the pressure the week is designed around.

# The inherited environment, as artifacts

The discovery phase gives the team a set of artifacts to examine, and the figures below are illustrative and meant to be tuned, but they should stay internally consistent once set. The application portfolio map shows the shape of the mess at a glance, a decades-old IBM i running order management and core plant systems in RPG with Aldon for change control, the stalled S/4 sitting beside it half-migrated, the connected-products platform running on a hyperscaler with its own data pipeline, and a scatter of competing BI tools feeding a data lake that's become a dumping ground. The thing the map reveals, to a team that reads it carefully, is that nothing has been retired, so the company is paying to run the old and the new at once.

The project dashboards tell the harder story. S/4 is red, roughly two years past its date and well past forty million against an original budget near twenty-five, stuck because the dependencies between the legacy systems and the new core were never fully mapped. The connected-products initiative is amber, three years in, telematics shipped on something like forty percent of the eligible fleet, annual run cost rising, monetization close to zero, and a rising count of dealer complaints about data access. The IT org chart carries its own signals, elevated attrition, a thin bench, and one or two people left who actually understand the IBM i codebase, which is a risk hiding in plain sight.

Two more artifacts give the team the why behind the what. The budget shows the digital spend stacked against the returns it was promised to deliver, which is the document Doug Reinhardt has clearly been losing sleep over. And Tom Bryce's original transformation deck is available to read, the vision he sold the board, all ambition and no sequencing, which is the single most useful artifact for understanding what went wrong, because the gap between what that deck promised and what the dashboards show is the whole lesson of the inheritance. A team that reads Bryce's deck against the dashboards understands the situation faster than a team that reads either alone.

# The executive reads

Each executive gives the team a perspective in the discovery phase, and each one is shaped by self-interest, so the reads conflict and the team has to weigh them rather than average them. Ray Calloway repeats the bold-and-vague mandate and watches to see whether the CIO presses him for specifics or fills the vacuum themselves, and the tell a sharp team catches is that he wants the data bet pursued but won't say so until someone hands him the cover, which is the thread that pays off in Week 2.

Doug Reinhardt is skeptical and direct, and he's been burned. He tells the CIO that the last person promised the world and lit forty million on fire, that he wants the bleeding stopped, and that he'll believe the digital story when someone shows him the numbers behind it. He isn't hostile so much as inoculated, and the team that brings him rigor instead of vision starts converting him early. Sharon Petrillo is the opposite kind of wary, protective of her floor and suspicious that IT will show up with a project and leave her with a production stoppage, and her message is essentially to stay off her operations until the CIO has earned the right to be there. How the team handles Sharon in Week 1 is the first deposit into the OT relationship that decides the Week 10 breach.

Carl Ferraro is the most persuasive person the team meets, and that's the trap. He's smooth and commercial and he gives a genuinely compelling read, that the dealers are the business, that the channel relationships are what actually make DigitalCo money, and that the smart play is to stabilize the core and not rock the boat with data ambitions that spook the dealers. It's articulate, it's confident, and it's self-serving, because what Carl is really protecting is his channel from the disruption the data strategy implies, and a team that anchors on the most convincing voice in the room gets steered away from the mandate. Lena Fischer adds the contested-turf dimension, proud of the machines and protective of the connected-products initiative she conceived as engineering features rather than a services business, and her posture in Week 1 tells the team whether the data-and-services strategy will have engineering as an ally or a rival. Gloria Tran is available but quiet, and a team that thinks to ask her learns early that the machine-data ownership question isn't only a strategy question but a legal one with real teeth, which is a thread most teams won't pull until it's pulling on them in Week 11.

# The advisors in Week 1

This is the advisors' debut, and Week 1 is Diane Brandt's week more than anyone's. She's the dominant voice, and she works entirely by question, asking the team what problem it's actually solving before it touches anything, who has to own the answer alongside the CIO, and whether the team has read what Calloway actually signaled rather than what he literally said. Her recurring caution is against motion mistaken for progress, "the board wants to see you do something, and the worst thing you can do right now is give them a something you'll regret in March." She's the coherence conscience from the first week, pressing the team to articulate a real thesis rather than a to-do list.

The others fill in their lanes. Marcus Webb cuts through the artifacts, explaining that S/4 stalled because nobody mapped what the legacy core was coupled to, that the team can't responsibly decide the migration's fate until it understands those dependencies, and asking of the connected-products platform what it actually runs on and what breaks when the fleet scales. Renata Voss is the quiet, crucial voice that's easiest to ignore, pointing at the factory-floor black box and the connected fleet as an unsecured attack surface and noting, without drama, that this exposure is being deferred again, which is precisely the early warning that separates the teams who survive Week 10 from the teams who don't. Daniel Stern brings the opportunity framing and a little of the temptation that comes with it, that the installed base is a genuine asset, that the rivals are becoming data companies, and that the mandate is clear enough to commit to the bold direction now, which is right about the destination and early about the timing.

Frank Delgado and Zoe Park round out the room. Frank reads the contracts Bryce left behind, the hyperscaler commitments under the connected-products platform and the integrator agreement on S/4, and flags where lock-in and bad terms are already lurking, which seeds the crises in Weeks 3, 4, and 7 without resolving anything yet. Zoe brings the future-state imagination, what the connected-products and data play could become and what rivals are reportedly piloting, with the energy that's valuable and the hype that has to be discounted. The team's real work in Week 1 is to hear all six without surrendering to any one of them, because leaning entirely on Daniel pulls toward premature commitment, leaning entirely on Diane pulls toward paralysis, and the synthesis is the skill.

# The decision

At the end of the thirty days the team submits four things, and together they're the most consequential deliverable in the sim because they set the baseline everything else is measured against. The first is a current-state assessment, the team's read of what's actually wrong, and the bar here is root cause over symptom, seeing that the real problem is a stalled and ungoverned digital bet sitting on a broken data foundation and an invisible factory floor with an IT organization that's lost the business's trust, rather than simply noting that S/4 is late. The second is a prioritization, what the team tackles first and what it defers, which forces a real choice under constraint.

The third is the strategy statement, and this is the coherence anchor for the entire arc. The team has to commit to a strategic thesis, and the engine holds them to it for fourteen weeks, so a sharp statement is a spine and a vague one is a quiet handicap. A strong statement says something specific and defensible, that DigitalCo's installed base is its differentiating asset and the company will become a data-and-services business along a stated path while protecting the channel and the core, and a weak statement says something that could be pasted into any company's deck, that the team will modernize IT and improve alignment and drive value, which gives the later weeks nothing to be coherent to. The fourth is an explicit posture on early action, because the board meeting is coming and Chen wants to see movement, so the team must decide whether to make an early visible move and which, or to signal that it's diagnosing before committing and show a credible thirty-sixty-ninety plan.

The tempting moves are laid in front of the team unlabeled, and three of them are traps. Killing the connected-products initiative looks decisive and would delight Reinhardt, but it forecloses the entire data-and-services strategy, contradicts Calloway's actual mandate, and hands Chen the verdict that the digital thesis failed. Committing to finish S/4 at all costs looks like resolve and pleases the CFO, but it repeats Bryce's mistake of committing before anyone understands why the thing stalled, and it pre-commits a Week 3 decision the team hasn't earned the right to make. Siding with Ferraro and slow-walking the data play keeps a powerful executive happy, but it misreads the mandate and surrenders the advantage the whole strategy depends on. The harder, sounder path is to diagnose before committing while making one low-risk credibility move that forecloses nothing, and the cleanest version is to commission the factory-floor visibility assessment with Sharon Petrillo, which builds the OT relationship, addresses the security through-line early, and demonstrates motion without betting the company, paired with a decision to stabilize rather than kill or finish S/4 while the dependencies get mapped. The students experience these as open options, and the scoring is what teaches the difference.

# Scoring Week 1

The submission runs across the four dimensions, and because there are no prior weeks, the coherence dimension here is about the quality of the anchor itself rather than consistency with anything earlier. Strategic Judgment rewards accurate root-cause diagnosis, the resistance of premature commitment, and the refusal to be captured by the loudest stakeholder, and it penalizes the three trap moves directly, marking down the team that kills connected-products, the team that commits to S/4 blind, and the team that adopts Ferraro's channel-protective read as its own. Execution and Consequence Management scores the early stakeholder handling, rewarding a team that builds credibility with Reinhardt through rigor and earns a first measure of Petrillo's trust, and penalizing a team that alienates Sharon or gets visibly captured by Carl, and it credits a sound thirty-sixty-ninety approach.

Coherence scores the strategy statement as an anchor, rewarding a thesis that's specific, defensible, and sharp enough to be held to, and penalizing the generic statement that commits to nothing, because that vagueness compounds across the whole sim as the team finds it has no spine to be consistent with. Deliverable Quality scores the assessment itself, and this is where the courses diverge most, with the graduate version expecting an executive memo that reasons to its conclusions and the undergraduate version expecting a structured assessment that names and applies the relevant frameworks. The trap detection isn't a separate mechanism, it's built into these four, so a team that fell for a trap sees it as a Strategic Judgment hit and, where the trap foreclosed options, as a consequence flag that follows it forward.

# What propagates forward

Week 1 seeds more of the arc than any other week. The strategy statement becomes the yardstick the coherence dimension measures against for thirteen weeks, so its sharpness or vagueness is a quiet tailwind or headwind the whole way. The early relationships become opening balances that later weeks draw on, Petrillo's trust feeding the OT gate that decides Week 10, the handling of Ferraro feeding the data-rights crisis in Weeks 8 and 11, the credibility built or lost with Reinhardt feeding every budget and board moment, and the posture toward Fischer feeding whether engineering supports the connected-products and platform decisions in Weeks 5 and 6.

The early-action posture sets the team's opening reputation, because a premature bold move that goes wrong dents credibility and spends Chen's patience, while a disciplined diagnosis builds standing but starts a clock of its own, since the board will want to see the diagnosis turn into results before long. And any premature commitment made here pre-loads later weeks in ways the team may come to regret, a team that committed to finishing S/4 walking into Week 3 having already made the call the week was meant to test. The world state the team hands itself at the end of Week 1 is the world it has to play the next thirteen weeks inside.

# MIS versus MISX in Week 1

The week is identical for both courses, and the scaffolding is the only thing that forks. MISX teams get the discovery phase with more guidance, clearer prompts toward the artifacts that matter, more explicit cues that the most persuasive executive may also be the most self-serving, and a decision framed with the strategic-thesis options more visibly drawn, so the undergraduates learn to read the situation with part of the map already sketched. MIS teams get the map blank, the executive self-interest unflagged, the traps unlabeled, and the strategy statement demanded without a template, so the graduate students do the diagnosis and the politics the way the actual job demands them. The advisors say the same things in the same voices to both, and the difference is only in how much the wrapper helps the team hear them.

---

# What this proves and what's next

Once Week 1 is built, the pattern is proven, and the remaining thirteen weeks become scripting against a working example rather than design from scratch, because each one reuses the five-beat shape, the same advisor voices, the same executive cast, and the same four-dimension scoring with its trap detection and its through-lines. The natural next build is the Week 2 script, the IT-and-business alignment confrontation, which is the first week the coherence dimension has something to measure against and the week Calloway's Week 1 subtext comes due, and after that the deliverable rubrics can be specified week by week now that there's a concrete example of what a week actually asks the team to produce.
