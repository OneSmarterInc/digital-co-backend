# DigitalCo Week 5: The Read
## Disruption Versus Dazzle, the Herd Test, and Telling Zoe's Signal From Her Hype

Week 5 follows the five-beat shape the earlier weeks established, so this script applies the pattern rather than re-explaining it. It opens Phase 2, and it turns the CIO outward for the first time, from the inherited mess and the foundational build to the emerging-technology landscape and the question of what to bet on. The week is built on a single uncomfortable truth, that the technology getting all the attention is rarely the technology that disrupts you, and it's the week where the innovation advisor's genuine signal and her hype finally have to be told apart, because following her uncritically and dismissing her entirely both end badly.

---

# The cold open

The week opens with a rival making noise. Meridian Industrial, a peer manufacturer in DigitalCo's space, takes the stage at the industry's big conference and very publicly bets the company on autonomous equipment and a sweeping digital-twin platform, complete with a slick keynote, an analyst chorus, and a press cycle that reaches DigitalCo's board within hours. The fear of falling behind lands immediately and from above, with Chen and Whitfield asking why DigitalCo isn't doing what Meridian just announced, and Calloway quietly wondering whether the company is about to be left behind. The pressure to respond is real, and it's pointed at the new CIO.

Underneath the noise, two other things are happening. Vendors are circling with dazzling demos of their own, autonomy pitches and digital-twin platforms that look extraordinary in a controlled room, and the same fear of missing out that Meridian triggered makes them land harder than they should. And almost silently, with no keynote and no press release, the genuinely disruptive technologies are building, additive manufacturing that lets customers and third parties print the parts DigitalCo sells, and edge AI cheap enough to let a low-end competitor ship a good-enough connected product at a fraction of the price. The task is to read the landscape and decide what to bet on, pilot, watch, or ignore, and the whole week turns on whether the team can tell the loud technology from the dangerous one.

---

# The state of play

The discovery phase puts the emerging-technology menu in front of the team, each item carrying its maturity, its relevance to DigitalCo's actual position, and its character as either sustaining or disruptive. Autonomy sits at the top of the menu because it's what Meridian bet on and what the vendors are demoing, and a clear-eyed look shows it's years from being real for DigitalCo's segment, capital-intensive, and arguably sustaining rather than disruptive, a better machine rather than a different game. Digital twins look almost as impressive in the demo and raise the question of whether they're a genuine capability or a feature wearing a platform's clothes, and whether DigitalCo even has the data foundation from Week 4 to make them real.

The artifacts that reward a careful reader are the quiet ones. Additive manufacturing looks incremental and boring, and it threatens a core revenue stream, because parts and aftermarket are where DigitalCo makes much of its margin, and a world where parts can be printed erodes both that revenue and the data-services hook the strategy is built on, which is also the seed of the right-to-repair fight that arrives in Week 11. Edge AI looks like more of the same and quietly enables low-end disruption, a cheaper competitor offering basic connected features good enough for customers who don't need the premium product, eating DigitalCo's position from below in the classic pattern. The disciplined read separates these two layers, the dazzling-but-sustaining-or-distant technologies that have everyone's attention from the boring-but-real disruptions that have none, and a team that reads the menu as a popularity contest reaches for exactly the wrong items.

---

# The executive and board reads

The board is the source of the pressure this week, and Chen and Whitfield carry it. Chen wants DigitalCo to be seen moving, and Meridian's announcement makes standing still look like weakness, while Whitfield, the digital conscience, is genuinely excited and inclined to champion the bold technological bet, which makes her an ally for engaging the future and a risk for amplifying the FOMO. Calloway's worry is more existential, the fear that the company is being out-innovated in public, and a team that can't give him a confident, reasoned read of why DigitalCo's path is sound will find him pulled toward chasing Meridian for the optics.

The countervailing voices come from the people closest to the work. Fischer, the engineer, has real opinions about autonomy and additive and is worth consulting precisely because she knows what's actually buildable and what isn't, and engaging her here also continues repairing or worsening the turf relationship from Week 4. Reinhardt is skeptical of expensive bets on unproven technology and provides useful gravity against the dazzle, though a team that hides behind his cost-caution can tip into dismissing everything. None of these reads is the answer on its own, and the week rewards a team that weighs the board's fear against the engineer's feasibility against the CFO's discipline rather than surrendering to any one of them.

---

# The advisors in Week 5

This is Zoe Park's week, and it's the week her two faces have to be told apart. She brings the future-state imagination and the energy, pressing the team to look past where each technology is today and ask what happens to the business when it matures, and her genuine value surfaces if the team listens for it, because she's the one most likely to see the boring disruptions early, to flag that additive and low-end competition are the real threats while everyone else stares at the autonomy demo. Her hype surfaces just as readily, the excitement about the dazzling autonomy and digital-twin bets that Meridian is chasing, and a team captured by Zoe follows the dazzle straight into misallocation. The skill the week teaches is separating her signal from her hype, taking her seriously enough to catch the real disruption and skeptically enough to discount the shiny one.

Marcus Webb is her counterweight, and the Marcus-versus-Zoe tension is the engine of the week. He's blunt about feasibility, "that autonomy demo is five years from real for our segment, and the digital twin is a feature dressed up as a platform," and his skepticism is the antidote to the dazzle. His own bias runs the other way, though, since the architect who distrusts the new can wave off a genuine disruption as a toy, so a team that follows Marcus uncritically risks the incumbent's complacency, dismissing the additive and low-end threats right alongside the autonomy hype. Daniel Stern supplies the lens that resolves the tension, the business-model question, "it isn't whether the demo is cool, it's whether it changes how we make money or how someone takes it from us," which points the team at the parts business and the premium position rather than the headlines, with his usual pull toward a bold bet to calibrate. Diane is lighter but pointed, asking whether the team is about to run its strategy on Meridian's press release and holding the coherence line, and Renata holds the through-line by noting that autonomous and connected equipment is itself an attack surface, so the security exposure follows the new technology too.

# The decision

The team submits an emerging-technology portfolio and a posture toward the rival's bet, with the deliverable scaling to the tier, an executive memo at the graduate level that reasons to the portfolio and defends it, and a structured analysis at the undergraduate level that applies the named lenses, disruptive versus sustaining innovation, hype-cycle reasoning, and real options, to reach the same calls. Three things have to be in it. The first is the portfolio itself, which technologies to bet on, pilot, watch, or ignore, with the disruptive-versus-sustaining rationale and a sense of how much to commit to each, structured as a real-options spread rather than an all-in or all-out gamble. The second is the innovation-capability decision, whether to stand up a separate innovation group or embed the work in the line. The third is the response to Meridian and the FOMO, whether to chase, ignore, or answer strategically by holding conviction and engaging the disruptions that actually matter.

The tempting moves sit in front of the team unlabeled, and the central one is the dazzle chased under herd pressure. Betting big on autonomy or the digital-twin platform because Meridian did and the board is nervous looks like keeping pace and lands as misallocation, pouring resources into a sustaining-or-distant technology while the real disruptions go unexamined. The opposite trap is the incumbent's complacency, dismissing all of it as hype and missing the additive and low-end threats that are genuinely coming, and the quiet version of that trap is reading only the loud items and never noticing that the boring technologies are the dangerous ones. There's a coherence trap too, because chasing Meridian's autonomy bet contradicts the data-services strategy the team has been building, a different and capital-intensive game that doesn't serve the data advantage, so a team that abandons its strategy for the herd takes the hit. The sounder path is a disciplined portfolio that bets modestly on defending against the real disruptions and engaging the low end, pilots or watches the dazzle without chasing it, holds conviction against the FOMO, and stays coherent with the strategy. The students experience these as open, and the scoring teaches the difference between reading the landscape and reacting to a rival.

# Scoring Week 5

The submission runs across the four dimensions, and this week leans on Strategic Judgment and the disruptive analysis inside it. Strategic Judgment rewards a disciplined separation of genuine disruption from hype and a real-options portfolio over an all-in or all-out posture, and it penalizes both the dazzle chased for FOMO and the blanket dismissal that misses the real threats. Execution and Consequence Management scores the handling of the board's fear, whether the team gave Chen and Whitfield a confident reasoned read or caved to the optics, and the innovation-capability decision and the conviction held under herd pressure. Coherence scores whether the read fits the data-services strategy and the Week 4 platform, so engaging the disruptions that threaten the advantage the team is building reads as coherent while chasing Meridian's autonomy bet reads as abandoning the strategy for the herd. Deliverable Quality rewards clean application of the disruption and real-options frameworks at the undergraduate tier and analytical depth on the disruptive read at the graduate tier.

The trap detection lives inside these four, with chasing the dazzle showing up as a combined Strategic Judgment and Coherence hit, dismissing everything as a Strategic Judgment hit for complacency, and missing the boring disruptions as a Strategic Judgment hit for reading the loud signals and ignoring the dangerous ones. The benchmark standing from Phase 1 shapes the pressure here in a way worth noting, because a team sitting behind feels the FOMO more sharply and is more tempted to chase the dazzle to catch up, which is exactly how the herd trap claims the teams that can least afford it, while a team near the top has the standing to hold conviction calmly.

# What propagates forward

Week 5 plants threads that pay off well downstream. A team that recognized the additive and parts threat is positioned for the right-to-repair and data-rights fight in Week 11, where that quiet disruption becomes a live crisis, and a team that registered the low-end threat carries a sharper read of its competitive position into the synthesis. The innovation-capability decision shapes how the team handles the technology choices ahead, most immediately the AI bet in Week 9, where a team with a real scouting capability engages more deliberately than one without.

The Meridian thread becomes a recurring presence rather than a one-week event. Its very public autonomy bet plays out across the arc, and a team that resisted the FOMO looks increasingly vindicated if that bet stumbles the way real industrial platform gambles often do, which mirrors the GE Predix lesson and rewards conviction in retrospect, while a team that chased Meridian is now tied to a rival's fate it doesn't control. The board relationship moves too, with a confident disciplined read building credibility with Chen and Whitfield and a panicked chase or a flat dismissal damaging it, and the coherence trajectory continues, with a strategy-consistent read reinforcing the through-line and a herd-driven pivot starting a contradiction the synthesis will find.

# MIS versus MISX in Week 5

The week is identical for both courses, and only the scaffolding forks. MISX teams get the frameworks named, disruptive versus sustaining innovation and hype-cycle reasoning and real options offered as the lenses, the technologies' disruptive character flagged more clearly with a hint that the boring ones are the real threats, and Meridian's bet marked as a herd-pressure test rather than a genuine signal, so the undergraduates learn to read disruption with the dangerous items pointed out. MIS teams get the disruptive-versus-sustaining judgment unscaffolded, so the students have to recognize that the boring additive threat is the real one on their own, and the FOMO unmarked, so they have to resist the herd without being told it's a test, and the decision framed open-ended, so the graduate students do the disruptive read the way the job demands. The advisors say the same things in the same voices to both, and the difference is only in how much the wrapper helps the team tell Zoe's signal from her hype.
