# DigitalCo Week 10: The Breach
## The OT Gate Detonates, the Compound Crisis, and the Week That Splits the Field

Week 10 follows the five-beat shape the earlier weeks established, so this script applies the pattern rather than re-explaining it. It's the single biggest leaderboard-mover in the sim, the week the security through-line either pays off or detonates, and it plays dramatically differently depending on what the team did in the nine weeks before it. The cruel irony at its center is that the breach comes through the connected fleet, the very asset the whole data-services strategy is built on, and the nightmare version is real, fielded customer machines that can be bricked or hijacked from a distance. This is the clearest demonstration in the sim of its core lesson, that coherent strategy across the whole arc beats handling each week in isolation, because here that difference is the difference between scars and catastrophe.

---

# The cold open

The breach announces itself over a short, terrible window. Ransomware propagates through DigitalCo's environment, encrypting systems and halting production at a plant, and then the attackers reveal the worse thing, that they've reached the connected fleet and can disable or manipulate fielded customer machines, with a ransom demand in cryptocurrency and a deadline, seventy-two hours, before they start bricking equipment in the field or leaking data. The thing the strategy was built on is the thing that's been turned against the company, and the customer machines that were supposed to generate advantage are now hostages.

How bad it is depends entirely on the nine weeks behind it, and the cold open makes that divergence visible immediately. A team that built OT posture, kept Petrillo as an ally, and caught the Week 7 signal detects the intrusion early and sees a containable incident, one plant affected and the fleet access caught before it spread far. A team that hit the OT gate, that treated the factory floor as a black box and security as a problem for later, finds the breach already everywhere before anyone noticed, multiple plants down and wide fleet access in the attackers' hands, with no visibility to even map the damage. And it isn't only the breach, because at the same moment a board member leaks the incident to the press and it goes public, a key security person resigns in the middle of the response, and the ransom clock runs. The task is to respond to all of it at once, and the week tests whether the team can run a disciplined incident response under a compound crisis, with a hand it largely dealt itself in the weeks before.

---

# The state of play

The discovery phase happens under crisis pressure, faster and more frantic than a normal week, and what the team can even see is determined by its OT visibility. For a prepared team, the incident state is mappable, the scope is known, the blast radius is limited, and the attack vector, the unaddressed fleet and floor exposure, is at least understood, which makes containment a real option. For a gated team, the incident state is mostly unknown, because the floor was never visible and the fleet was never secured, so the team is fighting a fire it can't see the edges of, and containment is barely available. The same breach is a manageable emergency for one team and a spreading catastrophe for another, and that difference was set long before this week.

The compound crisis is the same for everyone, and it's where even a prepared team can come undone. The breach, the public leak, the resignation, and the ransom deadline are four problems arriving at once, and they can't be handled one at a time, because the leak is shaping the public story while the team is still containing, the resignation is cutting response capacity while the clock runs, and the deadline is forcing the ransom decision before the scope is fully known. The response options are containment, where it's possible, disclosure to customers and dealers and regulators and the market, which is Tran's domain and legally constrained, and the ransom decision, which is the dilemma at the heart of the week. Paying funds crime, signals weakness, may carry sanctions exposure, and guarantees nothing, while refusing may leave plants down and customer machines bricked in the field, which is a safety and customer-relations disaster of its own, so there's no clean answer, only reasoning that's sound or reasoning that isn't.

# The executive and board reads

Petrillo is the read that the whole gate was about. The breach is in her plants and her fleet, and a team that built her trust over the prior weeks has an ally who can coordinate the floor response and move fast on the OT, while a team that alienated her or ignored the floor faces the crisis on the floor without her, blind and uncoordinated where it matters most. This is the moment the Petrillo relationship, which a team may have treated as optional back in Week 1, becomes the difference between containment and chaos. Tran is the read on disclosure, the one who frames what must be told to whom and by when, and whose counsel on the ransom's legal implications a team ignores at its peril.

The board read is a confidence crisis layered on the operational one. A board member has leaked, which means the board's own trust is fracturing in real time, and Hargrove, the swing whose confidence tracks performance, is forming the read that decides Week 13, while Ashby's heritage skepticism finds its grim vindication if the bold data-services bet turned out to be the attack surface that took the plants down. Reinhardt is watching an expensive disaster unfold, the downtime and the ransom and the remediation, and Calloway is managing his own exposure as the CEO who backed the strategy now being exploited. Ferraro carries the customer and dealer dimension, because a fleet compromise that bricks customer machines is a channel and customer catastrophe that lands on the relationships he's spent a career building, and it feeds straight into the data-rights crisis that follows. The week rewards a team that handles the people and the technical response together, and punishes a team that lets the compound crisis fragment its attention.

# The advisors in Week 10

This is Renata Voss's marquee, the crisis she's been pointing at since Week 1 finally here, and she leads without saying I told you so, because there's no time. She runs the incident response, the OT and IT containment, the mapping of what can be isolated and what's already gone, and she's clear-eyed that the team's options now are a function of the visibility it built, "this is the thing I've been flagging since the start, and here's how we contain it if we can see, and here's how bad it gets if we can't." Tran leads alongside her on the part that's legal rather than technical, framing the disclosure obligations and the ransom's implications, sanctions and regulatory exposure and precedent, and insisting that the ransom is not only an operational call. Marcus handles the technical reality underneath, what isolates, what's lost, the restoration path and how long it takes, the unglamorous engineering of getting back up.

Diane works the compound crisis itself, the thing that overwhelms teams that try to go in order. She's blunt that the breach and the leak and the resignation and the deadline can't be handled sequentially and demand triage, and she holds the communication line hard, "they'll forgive a breach handled well, they will not forgive a cover-up, so do not lie to the board now." Daniel names the strategic stakes, that the attackers compromised the foundation of the entire strategy and the customer impact is the strategic impact, which keeps the team from treating the breach as merely an IT event. Frank is lighter but pointed on the ransom mechanics, that engaging the attackers means engaging a professional negotiator and that paying guarantees nothing, and Zoe is minimal, because a crisis is not her week. The team's job is to run the response and the disclosure and the triage and the ransom decision together, under pressure, with the severity already set by the gate and the quality of the play still entirely in its hands.

# The decision

The team makes its incident-response decisions under crisis pressure, with the deliverable scaling to the tier, an executive crisis memo and response plan at the graduate level and a structured incident response at the undergraduate level that applies the named lenses, the incident response lifecycle, OT and IT security, crisis communication, and disclosure obligations. The decisions come together rather than in sequence. Containment, to the extent the team's visibility allows it, isolating what can be isolated and stopping the spread. Disclosure, what to tell customers and dealers and regulators and the market, framed with Tran and made transparent rather than spun, especially now that the leak has made concealment impossible anyway. The ransom decision, pay or refuse, with the reasoning that has to weigh the funding of crime and the sanctions exposure and the absence of any guarantee against the prospect of plants staying down and customer machines staying bricked. And the management of the compound crisis through triage, handling the breach and the leak and the resignation and the deadline in parallel rather than one at a time.

The tempting moves sit in front of the team unlabeled, and the largest one was set across the whole sim. Treating security as a Week 10 problem rather than a through-line is the trap that's detonating right now, and a team that hit the gate is defenseless not because of anything it did this week but because of nine weeks of deferral, which is the lesson at its starkest. Handling the compound crisis linearly is the in-week trap, the sequential response that gets overwhelmed because the problems won't wait their turn. Spinning or hiding the breach is the communication trap, fatal now that it's public. And the ransom decision is genuinely open, scored on the reasoning rather than the choice, because there is no clean answer and a thoughtful refusal and a thoughtful payment can both be defensible while a thoughtless version of either is not. The sounder path is a disciplined response that contains what it can, discloses transparently, triages the compound crisis in parallel, and reasons carefully to a ransom decision, executed with composure, which for a prepared team limits the damage to scars and for a gated team minimizes a catastrophe it can no longer avoid. The students experience all of this under pressure, and the scoring teaches that the breach was survivable for the teams that earned survival in advance.

# Scoring Week 10 and the gate detonation

The submission runs across the four dimensions, and this week leans hard on Execution and Consequence Management. Consequence Management scores the crisis handling, the containment, the disclosure, the compound-crisis triage, and the stakeholder and board management, and this is where the OT gate detonates, because a prepared team limits the blast radius and runs a disciplined response while a gated team faces a fleet-wide catastrophe with no visibility and no capacity. Strategic Judgment scores the quality of the decisions under pressure, the containment approach, the disclosure call, and the reasoning behind the ransom decision. Coherence scores whether the response holds together with discipline and transparency rather than fragmenting into panic, and it enforces the whole-sim security coherence the gate represents. Deliverable Quality rewards clean application of the incident-response and crisis frameworks at the undergraduate tier and analytical depth on the crisis response at the graduate tier.

The gate detonation is the defining event, and it operates at the scenario level as a ceiling rather than a point deduction. A team that built OT posture keeps its blast radius limited and survives the breach with scars, its endgame ceiling intact, with the in-week response determining where within that range it lands. A team that hit the gate takes catastrophic, fleet-wide damage and has its ceiling capped below the top tier exactly as Week 7 set it, with the in-week response able to minimize but not avoid the catastrophe. The field splits more dramatically here than anywhere in the sim, the security-coherent teams and the security-neglecting teams pulling visibly apart, and the ransom decision sits inside the scoring as a reasoning test rather than a binary, so a team is graded on how well it thought rather than which way it chose. This is the unmistakable demonstration that the sim was always about coherence, because the breach that overwhelms one team is the breach another team prepared for over nine quiet weeks.

# What propagates forward

Week 10 reaches forward into the rest of the sim with unusual force. The breach feeds Week 11 directly, because a fleet compromise that bricked customer machines is a customer and dealer trust catastrophe that compounds the data-rights crisis already loaded by Weeks 6 and 8, turning a contractual fight into a fight on top of a betrayal. It feeds Week 13 through the board's confidence, because a team that handled the breach well faces a board willing to listen while a team that face-planted faces a board that has watched the strategy blow up, with Hargrove's swing and Ashby's vindication both shaped here.

The endgame and the relationships move too. The gate has now capped the ceiling for the teams that hit it, which makes this the single largest endgame determinant in the sim, and the breach is expensive on top of everything, draining a budget already strained by earlier weeks. The customer and dealer relationships are damaged in proportion to the fleet impact, feeding the trust dimension of Week 11 and the synthesis, and the Petrillo relationship is either confirmed as the ally that saved the response or exposed as the absence that doomed it. The coherence trajectory resolves its security thread here, with a disciplined response reinforcing the through-line and a panicked one starting a contradiction, and the whole arc tilts toward its endgame on the strength of how this week went.

# MIS versus MISX in Week 10

The week is identical for both courses, the gate is the same scenario-level ceiling for both, and the scaffolding is what forks. MISX teams get the frameworks named, the incident response lifecycle and OT and IT security and crisis communication and disclosure obligations offered as the lenses, the response more structured with the steps drawn, the compound-crisis triage scaffolded with a prompt to handle the simultaneous problems in parallel, and the ransom dilemma framed with its considerations laid out, so the undergraduates run the crisis with a process to follow. MIS teams get the response unscaffolded, running the incident themselves under pressure, the compound crisis hitting harder with less guidance on how to triage, and the ransom dilemma fully open with no considerations drawn, so the graduate students exercise crisis judgment the way the job demands, which is realistic, because a real executive in a breach has no worksheet. The advisors say the same things in the same voices to both, and the difference is only in how much the wrapper helps the team keep its head while four crises run at once.

Week 10 detonates the gate, splits the field, and tilts the endgame on how the breach was handled. The natural next build is the Week 11 script, the customer data and privacy crisis, the hardest week to author because two earlier fuses converge there, the platform-openness choice from Week 6 and the data-rights posture from Week 8, now compounded by the customer betrayal of a fleet compromise, where the symmetry trap returns and the shared-value resolution is harder to reach than ever, and where the third benchmark closes Phase 3 by sorting the field on trust as well as advantage.
